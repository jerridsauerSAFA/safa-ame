The Four Forces

Name the four forces, their direction, and the point each acts through.

Vectors
  • A force is a push or pull. It is a vector: it has size and direction.
  • Lift – perpendicular (90°) to the flight path. Acts through the centre of pressure (CP).
  • Weight – straight down to the centre of the Earth. Acts through the centre of gravity (CG). Changes in flight as fuel burns.
  • Thrust – forward, in the direction the engine is mounted.
  • Drag – backward, opposite the flight path.
  • Balanced forces = steady cruise. Unbalanced = the aircraft accelerates toward the bigger force. Lift ≥ Weight, Thrust ≥ Drag.

Lift up, weight down, thrust forward, drag back. Level cruise = all four balanced.

Go deeper

Lift depends on the wing's size, shape and speed. Drag depends on shape, the air's "stickiness" (viscosity) and speed. Thrust depends on the engine type, how many, and the throttle. The CP and CG are not usually at the same spot — that gap is what the tail has to balance (Section 10).

How Lift Is Made

Explain lift two ways: Newton and Bernoulli.

F = m × a
  • Lift is a mechanical force: a solid body must be in contact with a fluid, with a difference in velocity. No air = no lift.
  • Newton: F = m × a. The wing turns the air downward; the air pushes the wing up.
  • Bernoulli: Total = Static + Dynamic (constant). Air over the curved top moves faster → dynamic ↑ → static pressure ↓ on top → higher pressure underneath pushes up.
  • Both are true at once: change the flow's speed or direction and you get a force.
Go deeper

Newton's second law written for air: F = m × ΔV ÷ Δt — the force equals how much air you move times how fast you change its velocity. A bigger wing moves more air; a faster wing changes its velocity more. That is why lift grows with wing area and with the square of speed.

Airfoil Vocabulary

Label an airfoil: chord, camber, leading/trailing edge, relative wind, angle of attack, CP, aerodynamic centre.

AC ≈ ¼ chord
  • Leading edge = front. Trailing edge = back.
  • Chord = straight line from leading edge to trailing edge.
  • Camber = how curved the airfoil is. More camber → more air turned → more lift.
  • Relative wind = airflow coming at the wing, opposite to the flight path.
  • Angle of attack (AOA) = angle between the chord and the relative wind.
  • Centre of pressure (CP) = the one point lift acts through. It moves as AOA changes.
  • Aerodynamic centre (AC) = point where the pitching moment does not change ≈ ¼ chord back from the leading edge.
Go deeper

The upper and lower surfaces don't have their pressure centres at the same spot along the chord, so the airfoil tries to rotate (a pitching moment about the lateral axis). The farther apart those two points, the bigger the moment — which is why the tail exists.

Angle of Attack, Stall & the Lift Equation

What increases lift? What happens past the critical angle?

L = CL · ½ρV² · A
L = CL · ½ρV² · A
  • CL = lift coefficient (shape + AOA, found in a wind tunnel) · ρ = air density · V = velocity · A = wing area.
  • Lift grows with the square of speed: double V → 4× lift.
  • AOA ↑ → lift ↑ … until the critical angle (≈ 15–18° for most wings) → airflow separates from the top → lift drops suddenly = stall.
  • Flaps and slats add area and camber for slow take-off and landing.
  • Altitude ↑ → density ↓ → lift ↓. Eventually the air is too thin to hold the weight = the aircraft's ceiling.

Lift climbs steadily with angle of attack, then falls off a cliff at the critical angle.

Go deeper

CL is worked out backwards: measure lift on a model in a wind tunnel, then CL = L ÷ (½ρV²A). It lumps together everything hard to calculate — shape, AOA, viscosity, compressibility. A stall is about angle, not speed: a wing can stall at any speed if the critical angle is exceeded.

Types of Drag

Name the two families of drag, the three kinds of parasite drag, and what causes each.

Total = Parasite + Induced
  • Parasite drag = all drag not from making lift. Three kinds:
  •   Skin friction – air rubbing on the surface (boundary layer). Rough surface ↑, smooth/waxed ↓.
  •   Form (pressure) drag – the body's shape blocking the flow. Fix = streamlining.
  •   Interference drag – airflows from two parts collide (wing–fuselage joint, struts).
  • Induced drag = "drag due to lift". Wingtip vortices → downwash → the lift vector tilts back.
  • Total drag = Parasite + Induced
Go deeper

The drag equation mirrors the lift equation: D = CD · ½ρV² · A. CD is harder to pin down than CL because it bundles form, friction, interference and induced drag, and engineers must choose which area to use (wing area, frontal area or total surface area).

Drag vs Speed

As airspeed increases, what happens to parasite drag, induced drag and total drag?

D = CD · ½ρV² · A
  • Speed ↑ → parasite drag ↑ (with V²). Fast = more air hitting everything.
  • Speed ↑ → induced drag ↓. Slow flight needs a high AOA → big vortices → lots of induced drag.
  • Total drag is a U-shape. Minimum total drag is where the two curves cross.
  • Drag also rises with density ↑ and AOA ↑.

Too slow: induced drag dominates. Too fast: parasite drag dominates. The bottom of the U is the most efficient speed.

Go deeper

This U-curve is the whole reason there is a "best glide" and "best endurance" speed — you will meet it again in Module 5. It also explains why a heavily loaded aircraft (more lift needed → more induced drag) has a higher minimum-drag speed.

Wingtip Vortices, Winglets & Ground Effect

Why do vortices form, what does a winglet do, and what is ground effect?

Within 1 wingspan
  • High pressure under the wing wraps around the tip to the low-pressure top → a swirling vortex. Downwash is strongest at the tip, weakest at the root.
  • Winglet = a small fin at the tip that blocks and redirects the vortex → less induced drag → more efficient wing, better fuel burn.
  • Ground effect = within one wingspan of the ground, the ground squashes the downwash → less induced drag, more lift.
  • Danger: come in too fast and the aircraft floats down the runway instead of landing.
Go deeper

A winglet has its own small tip vortex, but it forms high above the wing where it can't disturb the main airflow. Wake turbulence behind a heavy jet is these same vortices — strongest when the aircraft is heavy, slow and clean, which is why light aircraft wait behind airliners on departure.

Boundary Layer

What is the boundary layer, and what is the transition point?

Laminar → Turbulent
  • A very thin layer of air stuck to the surface by viscosity. Speed is zero at the skin and rises to full airflow speed a little way out.
  • Laminar = smooth, orderly layers near the front of the wing. Low friction.
  • Farther back it slows, thickens and becomes turbulent = rough, swirling. More friction.
  • Transition point = where laminar becomes turbulent. Rough surfaces move it forward.
  • The boundary layer gives the wing an "effective" shape slightly different from its real shape.
Go deeper

Keeping flow laminar as far back as possible is why laminar-flow wings are built so smooth, and why a bit of frost or bugs on the leading edge matters more than you'd think — it trips the layer early and adds drag.

Wing Design

Define planform, aspect ratio, sweep, dihedral and anhedral, and what each is for.

Shape = job
  • Planform = the wing's shape seen from above.
  • Aspect ratio = span compared to chord (long and narrow = high AR). High AR → less induced drag for the same lift. Gliders.
  • Biplane → lots of lift at low speed, lots of drag. Monoplane → less drag, faster.
  • Swept wing → for fast aircraft. Cuts drag near the speed of sound; the sweep "fools" the air into seeing a longer chord. Peak drag ≈ ¼ of a straight wing.
  • Dihedral = wings angled up. The low wing meets the air at a higher AOA → more lift → rolls back level. Roll stability.
  • Anhedral = wings angled down. Less roll stability, more manoeuvrable (fighters), or to cancel the pendulum effect of a high wing.
Go deeper

Every wing is a trade: the Cessna 150's high straight wing is stable and slow; a Challenger's swept low wing is fast but needs more runway. Aspect ratio is the cleanest lever on induced drag — which is why sailplanes have wings like blades.

Tail Design & the Three Axes

Which tail surface gives which kind of stability? Conventional tail vs T-tail?

Pitch · Yaw · Roll
  • Horizontal stabilizer → longitudinal stability = pitch (nose up/down) about the lateral axis (wingtip to wingtip).
  • Vertical stabilizer (+ rudder) → directional stability = yaw (nose left/right) about the vertical axis.
  • Wings + dihedral → lateral stability = roll about the longitudinal axis (nose to tail).
  • Conventional tail: the wing wants to pitch nose-down; the tail pushes the nose up to cancel it. CG too far forward or aft → unstable.
  • T-tail: stabilizer on top of the fin — out of the wing's wake and engine blast, so more predictable at low speed. Downside: heavier (fin must be stronger).
Go deeper

Tails borrow the same design ideas as wings — sweep, dihedral, anhedral — and their layout depends on where the engines and wing sit. Module 3 goes deep on stability; for now know which surface controls which axis.

Summary

Four forces act on an aircraft — lift and weight, thrust and drag — and level cruise means they balance. A wing makes lift by turning air downward (Newton) and by speeding air over its curved top so static pressure drops (Bernoulli); lift rises with wing area, density, angle of attack and the square of speed, until the critical angle where the flow separates and the wing stalls. Drag comes in two families: parasite drag (skin friction, form, interference), which grows with speed, and induced drag from wingtip vortices, which shrinks with speed — total drag is a U-curve. Winglets tame the vortices; ground effect within one wingspan cuts induced drag. Wing shape sets the job: aspect ratio, sweep, dihedral and anhedral trade efficiency, speed and stability. The tail finishes the design — horizontal stabilizer for pitch, vertical for yaw — with the T-tail trading weight for cleaner air.

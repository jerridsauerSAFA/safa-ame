Three Axes & Controls

Name each axis, the motion about it, the control surface, and the part that gives stability.

All cross at the CG
  • Longitudinal axis (nose → tail) → ROLL → ailerons → stability from the wings = lateral stability.
  • Lateral axis (wingtip → wingtip) → PITCH → elevator → stability from the horizontal stabilizer = longitudinal stability.
  • Vertical axis (up/down through the CG) → YAW → rudder → stability from the vertical stabilizer = directional stability.
  • All three axes cross at the centre of gravity.
  • Trap: the stability name is NOT the axis name. Lateral stability = roll about the longitudinal axis. Longitudinal stability = pitch about the lateral axis.
Go deeper

Say the memory line out loud until it's automatic — nearly every question in this module comes back to this table. The axis names describe the line; the stability names describe which way the aircraft is being held steady.

Static vs Dynamic Stability

What is the difference, and what do positive, neutral and negative mean for each?

First move · Over time
  • Static stability = the first tendency after a disturbance.
  •   Positive → starts back toward trim · Neutral → stays where it was put · Negative → keeps moving away.
  • Dynamic stability = what happens over time — do the oscillations shrink, stay, or grow?
  •   Positive → oscillations die out · Neutral → same size forever · Negative → grow bigger.
  • Aircraft are designed to be positive static and positive dynamic.
Go deeper

Fighters are built close to neutral so they turn fast; trainers and airliners are strongly positive so they fly hands-off. You can have positive static but negative dynamic stability: the nose starts back toward level every time, but each swing is bigger than the last.

Longitudinal Stability (Pitch)

What provides it, and how does the tail balance the wing?

Horizontal stabilizer
  • Provided by the horizontal stabilizer.
  • The wing's lift acts behind the CG → the wing wants to pitch nose-down.
  • The tail makes a down-load → pitches the nose up → balance, like a seesaw.
  • It only works if the CG is inside the published range — check the loading every flight.
  • CG too far forward or too far aft → longitudinally unstable.
Go deeper

Because the tail pushes down, it is actually "negative lift" the wing has to make up for — which is why a forward CG (more tail down-load needed) raises the stall speed. Canard designs (Section 10) flip this: the front surface lifts up instead.

CG Out of Limits

What happens with a CG too far forward? Too far aft?

Fwd = heavy · Aft = unstable
  • Too far FORWARD → nose-heavy → more tail down-load needed → higher stall speed, hard to flare on landing. Stable, but heavy.
  • Too far AFT → tail-heavy → unstable in pitch → poor stall and spin recovery. Light controls, but dangerous.
  • Rule: forward = stable but heavy; aft = light but unstable.
  • This is why Weight & Balance (Module 6) matters to an AME.
Go deeper

An aft CG is the more dangerous of the two because it can make the aircraft unrecoverable from a stall or spin. A forward CG mostly costs performance. Either way the aircraft is outside its certified envelope and must not fly.

Lateral Stability (Roll)

How does dihedral level the wings after a disturbance?

Wings · Dihedral
  • Provided by the wings.
  • Dihedral = wings angled upward from the horizontal.
  • A wing drops → the aircraft sideslips → the low wing meets the air at a higher angle of attack → more lift → rolls back level.
  • Other helpers: keel / pendulum effect (high wing, CG hanging below), and sweepback (the low wing shows more effective span → more lift).
Go deeper

Too much lateral stability is a problem too — it fights the pilot in turns and, combined with weak directional stability, causes Dutch roll (Section 7). High-wing aircraft often need little or no dihedral because the pendulum effect already does the job.

Directional Stability (Yaw)

What provides it? Why is the fin sometimes offset?

Vertical stabilizer
  • Provided by the vertical stabilizer (fin). Bigger tail area = more stable.
  • Weathercock effect: the nose yaws → airflow hits the side of the fin → pushes the tail back → nose swings back into the relative wind, like a weathervane.
  • Sweepback helps too: the forward-going wing has more drag and pulls the nose back.
  • Offset fin: the fin is angled slightly off the centreline so it lines up with the spiralling propeller slipstream → little or no rudder needed to fly straight.
Go deeper

The propeller throws air back in a corkscrew that hits one side of the fin, which is why single-engine aircraft yaw left on take-off and need right rudder. An offset fin (or an offset engine mount) builds the correction in at cruise power.

Dutch Roll & Spiral Instability

What causes Dutch roll, and how is it fixed?

Dihedral vs fin
  • Dutch roll = rolling and yawing at the same time — a wallowing motion.
  • Cause: lateral stability (dihedral effect) is stronger than directional stability.
  • Not dangerous — but passengers feel sick. Fix: a yaw damper (most large aircraft).
  • The opposite: directional stronger than lateral → spiral instability — a slow, diverging spiral dive if the pilot does nothing.
Go deeper

Most swept-wing jets lean toward Dutch roll (sweep adds a lot of dihedral effect), so they carry yaw dampers. Most light aircraft lean slightly spiral — which is safer, because a spiral is slow and easy to catch.

Adverse Yaw

Why does the nose yaw the wrong way when you roll?

Drag on the rising wing
  • Roll right: left aileron goes down → more camber → more lift on the left wing → it rises.
  • More lift also means more induced drag on that rising wing.
  • Extra drag on the rising (left) wing pulls the nose left — opposite to the roll. That is adverse yaw.
  • Rule: roll right → nose yaws left (unless corrected).
Go deeper

The pilot's fix is coordinated rudder — a touch of right rudder with a right roll. The designer's fixes are in the next section. Gliders, with long wings and big ailerons, show adverse yaw most strongly.

Four Fixes for Adverse Yaw

Name the four design fixes and how each one works.

Differential · Frise · Interconnect · Spoilers
  • Differential ailerons — the UP aileron travels farther than the DOWN one → extra drag on the descending wing balances most of the adverse drag.
  • Frise ailerons — the up-going aileron's nose pokes below the wing into the airflow → adds drag on the low wing.
  • Aileron–rudder interconnect — springs automatically move the rudder with the ailerons → roll right pulls the right pedal → cancels the left yaw.
  • Roll spoilers — a spoiler rises on the down-going wing → kills lift and adds drag on that side → no adverse yaw at all (large jets).
Go deeper

Most light aircraft use differential or Frise ailerons (or both). Airliners use roll spoilers plus small ailerons. The interconnect is common on aircraft with strong adverse yaw and light rudder forces, such as some Pipers.

Canards

Control canard vs lifting canard — and the advantages and risks of a forward surface.

Forward horizontal surface
  • A canard is a small horizontal surface ahead of the main wing.
  • Control canard — main wing carries the weight; canard sits near 0° AOA; used for pitch stability and control.
  • Lifting canard — shares the weight, generates an up load (no tail down-load) → less drag, higher aspect ratio; but no trailing-edge flaps on the wing.
  • Advantages: extra lift, balances the wing's pitching moment, less drag, sits out of the wing's downwash.
  • Risks: nose-up moment is destabilizing; the wing must sit behind the CG; the canard is designed to stall first → nose drops → momentary loss of pitch control.
Go deeper

Both surfaces lifting up is why canard aircraft such as the Rutan designs are so efficient — a conventional tail is dead weight pushing down. The price is careful CG control and the need to make the canard stall before the wing so the aircraft can never enter a deep stall.

Summary

An aircraft moves about three axes that cross at the CG — roll about the longitudinal axis (ailerons, steadied by the wings), pitch about the lateral axis (elevator, steadied by the horizontal stabilizer) and yaw about the vertical axis (rudder, steadied by the fin). Static stability is the first move after a disturbance; dynamic stability is what the motion does over time, and aircraft are built to be positive in both. Pitch stability depends on the tail's down-load balancing the wing — and on the CG staying inside its range: forward is heavy, aft is unstable. Dihedral levels the wings; the fin weathercocks the nose straight. Too much dihedral effect gives Dutch roll (yaw damper), too much fin gives a spiral. Rolling creates adverse yaw from the rising wing's drag, fixed by differential or Frise ailerons, interconnect springs or roll spoilers. Canards put the horizontal surface in front, lifting up instead of pushing down, at the cost of tricky stability.

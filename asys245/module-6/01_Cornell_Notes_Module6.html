The Boundary Layer

What is the boundary layer, and how does it give a wing an "effective shape" that is different from its real shape?

Sticky air
  • Air molecules right against a surface stick to it — their speed is zero. The molecules just above are slowed by bumping into the stuck ones, and they slow the layer above them, and so on.
  • The farther from the surface, the less the surface matters. The result is a thin layer where the air speed goes from zero at the surface up to the free-stream speed away from it. That layer is the boundary layer.
  • The airflow outside reacts to the edge of the boundary layer as if it were the surface. So every object has an effective shape that is slightly different from (slightly fatter than) its physical shape.
  • If the boundary layer separates (lifts off the surface), the effective shape becomes very different from the real one — and the wing stops working like a wing.
  • Keeping the boundary layer attached is what slots, slats, slotted flaps and vortex generators are all about (Module 4).

Above the dashed edge the air is at full free-stream speed. Below it, inside the boundary layer, the speed drops from full speed at the edge to zero at the skin. The outside flow "sees" that edge as the wing.

Go deeper

The boundary layer is why surface condition matters so much to an AME. Frost, dents, bad rivet lines or a rough paint repair thicken the layer and make it separate sooner — the wing stalls earlier and at a higher speed than the book says. On a small wing the boundary layer is only millimetres thick, which is why a strip of frost the thickness of sandpaper is enough to change the stall.

Stall & Stall Patterns

What causes a wing to stall, and why do designers want the stall to start at the wing root?

Root first
  • For a thin airfoil, lift is directly proportional to angle of attack at small angles (roughly +10° to −10°). More angle, more lift — in a straight line.
  • Past that, the boundary layer separates from the top of the wing. Lift drops abruptly and drag jumps. That is a stall. A stall is about angle of attack, not speed — the wing can stall at any speed if the angle is high enough.
  • Different wing shapes stall in different patterns: a rectangular wing tends to stall at the root first; tapered and swept wings tend to stall at the tips first.
  • The desirable pattern is root first: the tips, where the ailerons are, keep flying, so the pilot keeps roll control at high angles of attack and gets a warning (buffet) before the whole wing lets go.
  • A tip-first stall is nasty: one tip usually goes before the other, the aircraft rolls, and the ailerons are already in stalled air.

Designers want the stall to start at the root so the ailerons keep working and the pilot gets a warning.

Go deeper

Why does a rectangular wing stall at the root? Its tip vortex spills around the tip and actually reduces the local angle of attack out there, so the tip is the last part to reach the critical angle. A tapered wing has a weaker tip vortex and a higher local lift coefficient near the tip, so the tip goes first.

Vortex Generators

What is a vortex generator, and how does a tiny fin delay the stall?

Energize the layer
  • Vortex generators (VGs) are small fins in a row along the top of the wing (sometimes on the tail too).
  • As air flows back across the wing it loses energy in the boundary layer. As it loses energy it starts to separate — and separation is a stall.
  • Each VG spins off a small vortex. The vortex mixes the fast, high-energy air from outside the boundary layer down into it, re-energizing the slow air near the skin.
  • Result: the boundary layer stays attached to a higher angle of attack → the wing is less likely to stall, the stall speed drops, and the controls keep working longer.

A small fin spins off a vortex that stirs fast air into the tired boundary layer, so it stays attached longer.

Go deeper

Look at the top of a Cessna 180 or a Bonanza with a STOL kit: a neat row of little aluminum tabs about a third of the way back from the leading edge. Each one is only a few centimetres tall — about the thickness of the boundary layer — and that is the point. It only needs to reach the fast air just outside the layer to drag some of it down. For the AME, VG kits come with an STC: position, spacing and count are fixed by the approval, and a missing VG is a snag, not a cosmetic fault.

Mach Number & the Speed of Sound

What is Mach number, what is critical Mach number, and why does the speed of sound change with altitude?

Speed of sound
  • Mach number = the aircraft's speed divided by the speed of sound. Mach 0.5 = half the speed of sound; Mach 2 = twice it.
  • The "speed of sound" is really the speed at which a small disturbance (pressure wave) travels through the air. Sound itself is just what the brain makes of those waves.
  • In the standard atmosphere at sea level it is about 761 mph (about 661 knots). It depends on air temperature — colder air = slower speed of sound. So at high altitude, where it is cold, an aircraft reaches a given Mach number at a lower true airspeed.
  • Critical Mach number (Mcrit) = the aircraft speed at which the air flowing over the wing first reaches Mach 1. The air over the top of a wing is faster than the aircraft, so the wing goes supersonic locally before the aircraft does.
  • Below Mcrit every bit of flow over the surface is subsonic. At Mcrit the fastest spot on the top surface just touches Mach 1.
  • History: 14 October 1947 — Capt. Chuck Yeager flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 "Glamorous Glennis" past Mach 1, air-launched from a B-29. Bullet-shaped fuselage, thin strong wings, and an adjustable horizontal stabilizer made it possible.

The aircraft can still be well below Mach 1 (about 0.8) when the air over its wing first hits Mach 1 — that is critical Mach.

Go deeper

Why temperature and not altitude? A pressure wave moves by molecules bumping their neighbours, and warm molecules move faster, so the wave travels faster. Cold air slows it. At 35,000 ft on a standard day the speed of sound is only about 575 kt, against about 661 kt at sea level — so an airliner cruising at Mach 0.82 is doing roughly 470 kt true. That is also why the X-1 was flown high: colder air meant Mach 1 arrived at a lower, safer true airspeed.

Shock Waves, Transonic Flight & Shock Stall

What is a shock wave, what changes as air passes through one, and what happens to drag in the transonic range?

Wave drag
  • A shock wave is a very thin wall in the air where the flow jumps from supersonic to subsonic. A normal shock wave stands at right angles (perpendicular) to the surface.
  • As air passes through a shock wave: pressure increases, temperature increases, velocity decreases (and density increases — the air is suddenly squeezed).
  • Shock waves form in stages: first on the top surface only (A), then also on the bottom (B), and finally the whole airfoil is supersonic with a bow wave ahead of it (C). The pressure jump reaching the ground is the sonic boom.
  • Transonic flight = the transition from subsonic to supersonic, from about critical Mach (≈ 0.8) to about Mach 1.3. Some of the flow is subsonic and some supersonic at the same time.
  • Drag increases greatly in the transonic range. Energy is dumped into the air as heat and pressure jumps, and the engine has to make up for it. This extra drag is called wave drag. The Mach number where the drag rise really begins is the critical drag Mach (Mcrd).
  • Shock stall = the shock wave separates the boundary layer behind it. Lift is lost and buffet starts, even though the angle of attack is low. A stall caused by speed, not by angle.

Shock waves (orange) appear on top first, then below, then a bow wave ahead once the whole airfoil is supersonic.

Go deeper

Think of the shock wave as a traffic jam. Supersonic air cannot "hear" the wing coming — pressure information travels at the speed of sound, and the air is moving faster than that — so it piles into the obstacle without warning and slams to a slower speed in a wall a few molecules thick. Behind that wall the air is hotter, denser and slower, and that sudden pressure jump pushes the boundary layer off the surface. Early jets that got too fast hit shock stall and "Mach tuck": the shock moved aft, the centre of pressure moved with it, and the nose dropped into a dive the elevator could not fix.

Wings for High-Speed Flight

Name two wing design features that suit transonic and supersonic flight, and say what each one does.

Sweep · Thin
  • Sweepback: angling the wing back reduces the effective Mach number along the chord. Only the part of the airflow that runs straight across the wing (chordwise) counts for shock formation; the spanwise part is "wasted". A swept wing can therefore fly faster before reaching critical Mach.
  • Thin wings with sharp leading edges: a blunt nose pushes a bow wave out ahead of it (lots of drag). A sharp leading edge lets the bow wave attach to the leading edge and become an oblique shock wave.
  • When the shocks are oblique (slanted), all the air around the wing stays supersonic and smooth — far less wave drag than a detached normal shock.
  • Example: the Lockheed F-104 Starfighter — a high-altitude interceptor with tiny, razor-thin, sharp-edged wings; the first US jet fighter to fly at twice the speed of sound. NASA used it as a chase plane.

Sweep lowers the Mach number the wing "feels"; a sharp, thin leading edge turns the bow wave into an attached oblique shock.

Go deeper

Sweep is a trick of geometry. If a wing is swept 35°, the airflow crossing the chord is the free stream multiplied by cos 35° — about 82%. So the wing "feels" Mach 0.82 when the aircraft is at Mach 1.0. That is why nearly every airliner has 25–35° of sweep: it buys cruise speed without the brutal low-speed handling of a true supersonic wing. The F-104 went the other way — almost no sweep, but a wing so thin and sharp that ground crews fitted guards on the leading edges.

Summary

Air sticks to a surface and forms a boundary layer; when it separates the wing stalls, ideally root first so the ailerons keep working; vortex generators delay separation. Mach number is speed divided by the speed of sound, which depends on temperature; at critical Mach the flow over the wing first hits Mach 1, shock waves appear (pressure and temperature up, velocity down), wave drag rises sharply through the transonic range, and shock stall can follow. Sweepback and thin sharp wings are the designer's answers.

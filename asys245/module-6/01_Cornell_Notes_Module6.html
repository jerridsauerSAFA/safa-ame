Why Weight & Balance Matters

What does too much weight do to an aircraft, what does a CG too far forward or aft do, and who is responsible for which W&B report?

AME = empty · Pilot = loaded
  • Most modern aircraft are designed so that with every seat, the baggage bay and the tanks all full, they are overloaded. Every flight is a trade: full load means less fuel and range; full range means leaving people or bags behind.
  • Overloading hurts everything: higher take-off speed and longer take-off run; lower rate and angle of climb; lower service ceiling; slower cruise; shorter range; poorer manoeuvrability; higher landing speed and longer landing roll; and extra stress on the structure, especially the landing gear.
  • Balance (where the CG sits) decides stability and control. CG too far forward: the aircraft may not rotate for take-off; longitudinal control is reduced. CG too far aft: the aircraft may over-control in pitch; longitudinal stability is reduced (Module 3).
  • It is illegal to fly an aircraft outside its allowable weight and balance envelope.
  • Two reports, two owners: the AME calculates and provides the empty weight and empty CG; the pilot calculates the loaded weight and loaded CG for each flight from the AME's numbers.

The CG must sit inside the envelope. Forward of it the aircraft is nose-heavy and may not rotate; aft of it the aircraft is unstable.

Go deeper

This is the module where the AME's arithmetic goes straight into the pilot's hands. Every loaded W&B a pilot does starts from the empty weight and empty CG you signed for. A wrong number in the hangar is a wrong number on every flight until the next weighing — which is why the regulations treat the W&B report as a required technical record, not a nice-to-have.

Datum, Arm, Moment, CG

Define datum, arm, moment and centre of gravity, and give the sign convention for arms.

The four words
  • Datum — an imaginary vertical plane from which all horizontal measurements are taken, with the aircraft in the level flight attitude. Where is it? Wherever the manufacturer says — find it in the AMM, POH or TCDS. Common datums: the firewall, the spinner, the nose.
  • Arm — the horizontal distance (inches) from the datum to an item. It is a distance. Fuselage stations (FS) are numbered by their distance from the datum.
  • Sign convention: aft of the datum = positive (+); forward of the datum = negative (−). Put the datum at the nose and every arm is positive.
  • Side to side uses butt lines (BL) from the centreline (BL 0): right = +, left = −.
  • Moment — weight × arm (lb·in). Keep the signs: a positive weight at a negative arm is a negative moment; a removed item (negative weight) at a negative arm is a positive moment.
  • Centre of gravity (CG) — the point where the nose-heavy and tail-heavy moments are exactly equal: the balance point. It can be written as inches from the datum, a station number, or a percentage of MAC.

Everything is measured from the datum. Aft is +, forward is −. Moment = weight × arm, signs included.

Go deeper

Why not just put the datum at the CG? Because the CG moves every time you load the aircraft. The datum is a fixed reference the manufacturer chose once, so every arm in every manual, STC and equipment list is measured from the same place for the life of the type. Check the datum before you check anything else — an arm from the wrong datum is wrong in every row of the table.

The Principle: Moments Sum to Zero

State the first principle of weight and balance and show how it gives the formula for the CG.

CG = ΣM ÷ ΣW
  • First principle: the sum of the moments about any point of a stationary object is zero. That is what "balanced" means.
  • Teeter-totter: a 25 lb child and a 50 lb child on a 12 ft plank. Datum at child A. Moments: 25 × 0 = 0 and 50 × 12 = 600. The pivot must carry 75 lb at Y where 75 × Y = 600 → Y = 8 ft from child A. Trial and error works on a teeter-totter — not on a 737.
  • So the rule: CG = total moment ÷ total weight
  • Use the four-column table: item · weight · arm · moment. Work out each moment (weight × arm), then add the weights and add the moments, then divide.
  • Never add the arms. The CG is a "master arm" that comes out of the division, not a sum.
  • Deck example: 52,900 lb·in ÷ 495 lb = 106.9" aft of the datum.

Total moment 600 lb·ft divided by total weight 75 lb puts the balance point 8 ft from the lighter child.

Go deeper

The teeter-totter and the aircraft are the same problem. On the aircraft the "children" are the scale readings under each wheel, the "pivot" is the CG you are looking for, and the ground pushing up on the wheels is the negative total weight that makes the moments sum to zero. Spreadsheets do the multiplying, but you still have to get the signs right and resist adding the arm column.

Weight Terms

Define empty weight, useful load, and the maximum ramp, take-off, landing and zero-fuel weights.

Useful load = max gross − empty
  • Maximum (gross) weight — the most the aircraft and everything in it may weigh in flight. Found in the Type Certificate Data Sheet (TCDS) / Aircraft Specifications.
  • Maximum ramp (taxi) weight — the heaviest it may be on the ground. Maximum take-off weight — the heaviest at the start of the take-off roll. The difference is the fuel burned starting and taxiing.
  • Maximum landing weight — the heaviest it may land at (on a wide-body it can be 100,000 lb or more below take-off weight). Maximum zero-fuel weight — the heaviest with no usable fuel; anything above it must be fuel.
  • Empty weight — the aircraft with everything that is fixed in place and installed: airframe, powerplant, required and optional equipment, fixed ballast, full engine coolant, hydraulic fluid, residual (unusable) fuel, oil, radios. Empty weight CG = where it balances in that condition.
  • Useful load = maximum gross weight − empty weight. That is everything you can legally add: crew, passengers, baggage, usable fuel. Aircraft certified in both normal and utility categories may list two useful loads.
  • Minimum fuel — the fuel for about 30 minutes at cruise power; used in W&B calculations where the load is otherwise critical.

Useful load is what is left after the empty weight — and it is often less than people expect.

Go deeper

The deck's practice aircraft has a maximum gross weight of 1,670 lb and an empty weight of 1,176 lb, so the useful load is only about 494 lb. Put a 250 lb pilot in and 244 lb is left for passengers, bags and fuel — and fuel is 6 lb per gallon. That is why "full tanks, full seats, full baggage" is an overload on most light aircraft, and why the pilot's loaded W&B is not optional.

The W&B Report & the Rules

What does the empty weight and balance report contain, which regulations require it, and when must an aircraft be reweighed?

CAR 605.92 · Std 571 App C
  • Every aircraft leaves the factory with a weight and balance report in its records. It gives the empty weight and the empty weight CG, and must be current and accurate.
  • CAR 605.92 — the owner must keep an empty weight and balance report that meets Chapter 571 of the Airworthiness Manual (Standard 571 Appendix C: Aircraft Weight and Balance Control).
  • Standard 625 Appendix C — large aircraft are reweighed every five years (unless on an approved fleet W&B control program), or after major work — including paint, which weighs a lot.
  • Private / small aircraft — the factory report is amended when equipment is added or removed; a full reweigh is not required unless the records are lost.
  • Reweigh when: the report is lost; the paper trail back to the last weighing is broken; or a change cannot be calculated accurately.
  • A new report or amendment is needed after any change to empty weight or CG (Std 571 App C (2)(a)) — floats, skis, radios, seats. Practically, hardware like washers, screws, clamps and lockwire does not.

Known weight and arm → calculate an amendment. Unknown, or the paper trail is broken → reweigh.

Go deeper

"Including paint" surprises people. A full repaint on a light aircraft can add 10–20 lb, and on an airliner hundreds of pounds, all of it spread over the skin where the arm is long. That is why a large aircraft's five-year reweigh clock is reset by major work, and why a stripped-and-repainted aircraft is weighed rather than calculated.

Weighing: Equipment & Preparation

What scales are used, how is the aircraft levelled, and what must be done before the readings count?

Level · Clean · Closed hangar
  • Mechanical (beam) scales — like a doctor's scale: slide the weight until the bar floats between the stops. Usually only to 300 lb, and accuracy is a concern.
  • Electronic load cells — platform type (tire sits on the platform) or top-of-jack type (cell built into the jack). At least three scales or load cells; the readings are the scale weight.
  • The aircraft must be in the level flight attitude: use the levelling means from the TCDS / AMM / POH (e.g. Cessna 185: datum = front face of firewall, levelling means = upper door sill) with a spirit level, or a plumb bob from the wing leading edge. Jack or deflate tires until level.
  • Why level? Tail low → CG appears further aft than it is; tail high → CG appears too far forward.
  • Weigh in a closed hangar, fans off, no air currents; outside only if wind and moisture are negligible. Aircraft clean and dry — remove dirt, oil, grease, moisture.
  • Configure per the manual: flaps, spoilers, slats as instructed; all equipment on the certificated list installed and in place; baggage bays empty; items not normally carried removed; inspection plates, caps, cowlings, doors and exits fitted and in flight position. Hydraulic systems full; drinking water and lavatory tanks drained (unless the manufacturer says otherwise).
  • Tare weight — chocks, blocks or stands sitting on the scales are subtracted from the scale reading.

At least three weighing points, level per the TCDS, in a closed hangar with the fans off.

Go deeper

Levelling is where most weighing errors come from. The arms in the manual are measured with the aircraft level; tilt it and the wheels are no longer under the stations the manual assumes, so the moments are wrong even though the scales are right. Small nose-wheel aircraft are often levelled by taking the nose wheel off and resting the fork on the scale — then the removed wheel is weighed separately and added back in the table.

Fuel, Oil & Standard Weights

How much fuel and oil is in the empty weight, what are the three fuel conditions for weighing, and what are the standard fluid weights?

6 · 6.7 · 7.5 · 8.35 lb/gal
  • Empty weight includes only residual (unusable) fuel. So weigh in one of three conditions: (1) no fuel at all — then add the unusable fuel's weight, arm and moment mathematically; (2) only residual fuel in tanks and lines; (3) tanks full — then subtract the usable fuel mathematically.
  • Fuel weight depends on density: use a hydrometer to measure it when weighing with full tanks.
  • Oil: for aircraft certified since 1978, full engine oil is normally part of the empty weight — confirm in the TCDS. If the TCDS says only residual oil, either drain to residual or subtract the extra oil mathematically.
  • Standard weights when the exact weight is not known: aviation gasoline 6 lb/gal · turbine fuel 6.7 lb/gal · lubricating oil 7.5 lb/gal · water 8.35 lb/gal.
  • Crew and passengers: use the current standard weights in the TC AIM (TP 14371) unless actual weights are known.
  • Constant-speed-drive oil tanks full; hydraulic reservoirs full; water and lavatory tanks drained — unless the manufacturer says otherwise.

The four standard fluid weights. People: from the TC AIM.

Go deeper

Fuel density changes with temperature and type, which is why a hydrometer matters when you weigh with full tanks — on a 40-gallon aircraft a 5% density error is 12 lb sitting at the fuel arm. The 1978 oil rule exists because older type certificates were written when oil was treated as a consumable; read the TCDS note rather than assuming.

Worked Weighing: Empty Weight & CG

Walk through the steps of weighing an aircraft and the six-column table, using the practice example.

1176.37 lb @ 30.90"
  • Steps: read the manufacturer's weighing procedure → load/configure as specified → clean, dry, fans off → level on the scales → weigh → weigh the tare items → find the arms from the weighing points to the datum (wheel centrelines / jack points, from the AMM) → calculate → build the W&B report.
  • Practice aircraft (datum ahead of the nose wheel): right main 423 lb @ FS 46.81; left main 415 lb @ FS 46.81; nose 331 lb @ FS −10.00. Weighed with no fuel (add unusable fuel 9.00 lb @ FS 40.00), full oil that must come out (−13.13 lb @ FS −14.70), and the nose wheel assembly removed to level it (add 11.50 lb @ FS −10.00).
  • Moments: −3,310.00 · +19,800.63 · +19,426.15 · +193.01 (negative × negative) · +360.00 · −115.00.
  • Totals: weight 1,176.37 lb, moment 36,354.78 lb·in. CG = 36,354.78 ÷ 1,176.37 = 30.90" aft of the datum.
  • Max gross 1,670 lb → useful load = 1,670 − 1,176.37 = 493.63 lb.
  • Sanity check: the CG sits between the nose wheel (−10) and the mains (46.81), and closer to the mains because they carry more weight. If your CG is outside the stations, something is wrong.

The practice table from the deck. Removed items get a negative weight; two negatives make a positive moment.

Go deeper

Notice the oil row: −13.13 lb at −14.70" gives +193.01 lb·in. Taking weight out of the nose moves the CG aft, so the moment has to go up — the signs are doing the physics for you. If a removed item ever gives you a moment in the wrong direction, you have a sign error. The test question is usually exactly this table: fill it in, total it, divide.

Lateral CG & Helicopters

How is lateral CG calculated, and why is CG more critical on a helicopter?

Butt lines · Cyclic authority
  • Lateral CG = the balance point left or right of the longitudinal axis. Arms are butt lines from BL 0 (the centreline): right +, left −.
  • Same table, same rule. Practice aircraft: nose 331 lb @ BL 0 → 0; right main 423 lb @ +39.2 → +16,581.6; left main 415 lb @ −39.2 → −16,268.0. Total moment +313.6 ÷ 1,176.37 lb = +0.266" (right). Barely off centre.
  • Helicopters have a CG just like aeroplanes, but it is more critical and more limited. The limits are set by cyclic control authority — is there enough cyclic left to control the aircraft in every flight condition?
  • A helicopter needs both a longitudinal CG envelope and a lateral offset moment limit, so lateral W&B is a routine calculation there (Bell 206 levelling pads are on the transmission deck, right side).
  • Empty-weight CG ranges are rare for aeroplanes but common for helicopters.

Lateral CG uses butt lines instead of fuselage stations — same table, same division.

Go deeper

Why does a helicopter care so much? The rotor disc tilts to move the aircraft, and the cyclic has a fixed amount of travel. If the CG is too far forward, the pilot runs out of aft cyclic in a hover and the nose keeps dropping; too far sideways and there is no lateral cyclic left in a crosswind. A fixed-wing aircraft has a tail that resists; a helicopter has only the cyclic.

CG Range, the Envelope & MAC

What is the CG range, how do the limits change with weight, and how is CG expressed on large aircraft?

% MAC
  • The CG range is the span between a forward limit and an aft limit (both arms) inside which the aircraft must balance. The operating CG range applies to the loaded, ready-for-flight aircraft — every aircraft has one. An empty-weight CG range is listed only for some types (mostly helicopters); otherwise the TCDS says "none".
  • The limits can change with weight. Piper Seneca (gear down): fwd limit +86.4" at 4,000 lb, +82.0" at 3,400 lb, +80.7" at 2,780 lb; aft limit +94.6" throughout; straight-line variation between points. Retracting the gear changes the moment (−32 lb·in). Normal and utility categories may each have limits.
  • Checking: use the most current W&B report and the equipment list to find the moments, work out the new CG, then plot it on the CG envelope chart. Outside the limits = the aircraft cannot fly. Quick check: if your CG lands outside the station diagram, something is wrong.
  • Large aircraft (business jets to jumbos) give the CG as a percentage of the Mean Aerodynamic Chord (MAC) — the chord of an imaginary airfoil with the same aerodynamic behaviour as the real wing. It is useful for loading passengers and cargo.
  • The TCDS lists the MAC's leading and trailing edges from the datum: MAC = TEMAC − LEMAC. Then % MAC = (CG − LEMAC) ÷ MAC × 100. Example: 78" chord, CG range 15.6"–28.08" aft of the leading edge = 20%–36% MAC.
  • This ties back to longitudinal stability (Module 3): the CG sits ahead of the wing's centre of lift.

Plot weight against CG. The forward limit often moves aft as weight goes up; outside the shape the aircraft cannot legally fly.

Go deeper

Why does the forward limit move aft at high weight? At high weight the elevator has to produce more tail force to rotate and flare; letting the CG sit further forward would ask for more than the elevator can give. The aft limit is about stability and rarely changes. For airliners, % MAC lets a load controller compare any aircraft of the type on one chart, which is why cargo and passenger loading systems work in % MAC rather than inches.

Equipment Changes: Amendments

After an equipment change, how do you find the new empty weight and CG without reweighing, and what must be recorded?

Calculate · Log · Supersede
  • Any equipment change or alteration → a new empty weight and CG, either by weighing or by calculating. Calculating is acceptable when the exact weight and arm of every change is known (from the STC, AMM or the part).
  • An amendment = a W&B report whose new empty weight and CG are computed, not weighed. Row 1 is always the previous report (its weight, CG and moment, with its date — the factory report is amendment 0). Then removed items as negative weights, added items as positive. Total the weights and moments; divide.
  • Deck example: 2,350 lb @ +24.7"; install radio 5.8 lb @ −28", GPS 7.3 lb @ −26", ELT 2.8 lb @ +105"; remove strobe 1.4 lb @ +75", ADF 3 lb @ −28", seat 34 lb @ +60". Result: weight down 22.5 lb, CG forward 0.67". (The ADF, removed from a negative arm, gives a positive moment.)
  • Floats: 1,250 lb @ 27.2" (moment 34,000); remove main gear 132 lb @ 20" and tailwheel 20 lb @ 200"; add floats 308 lb @ 24" (from the STC). New: 1,406 lb @ 24.72". No reweigh needed.
  • Skis practice: 1,176.38 lb @ 30.91"; remove wheels/tires 22.8 lb @ 46.81" and nose wheel 11.5 lb @ −10"; add main skis 62 lb @ 42.5" and nose ski 25 lb @ −12.3". New: 1,229.08 lb @ 30.70"; useful load 440.92 lb.
  • Std 571 App C: whoever made the change enters it in the journey log before the next flight: (i) description of the change, (ii) effective date, (iii) weight and arm of each item installed or removed. Transcribe it to the W&B report. Mark the old report "Superseded" with date and signature and keep it — if the paper trail back to the last weighing is broken, the aircraft must be reweighed.

An amendment starts from the previous report, removes with negative weights, adds with positive, and divides.

Go deeper

The journey-log entry is the legal part. The amendment is only as good as its chain: factory report → amendment 1 → amendment 2 → today. Each one must reference the one before it, and each superseded page stays in the records. Lose one link and nobody can prove what the aircraft weighs, so it goes back on the scales. That is also why "temporary removal for repair" does not need an amendment — the item is coming back.

Addendums, Ballast & the Equipment List

What is an addendum, what is ballast and how do you place it, and what goes on the equipment list?

Arm = moment ÷ weight
  • Addendum = one of several empty W&B configurations kept inside one report or amendment. Wheels in the fall, floats in the spring? Don't write a new amendment each time — keep a float addendum and a wheel addendum. When the configuration changes, make a log entry saying which addendum is in effect. Same idea for cargo/passenger, medevac, camera fits.
  • Ballast = weight added on purpose at a chosen location to bring the CG into the envelope. Fixed ballast is bolted in by the AME and needs a W&B change; removable ballast is put in by the pilot before flight (water ballast in sailplanes).
  • Where to put it: you need a moment equal and opposite to the one you are cancelling, so arm = moment ÷ weight. Example: a 90 lb system with a net moment of +550 lb·in and a spare 50 lb weight: arm = −550 ÷ 50 = −11" — 11" forward of the datum. The longer the arm, the more effect per pound; close to the CG it does almost nothing.
  • Equipment list — required by Std 571 App C (1)(a): every item not part of the type design (fire extinguisher, transponder, engine heater, wheel pants, floats) listed with its weight, arm and moment. It is part of the W&B report; on light aircraft it is often in the POH / AFM. Before weighing, check the aircraft matches the list — fix one or the other.
  • Review: empty W&B = AME; loaded W&B = pilot; datum and levelling = POH / TCDS / AMM; moment = weight × arm; moments about a stationary object sum to zero; CG = ΣM ÷ ΣW; amendment = calculated update; addendum = one of several configurations.

Ballast works by moment: pick the weight, solve for the arm — or pick the arm, solve for the weight.

Go deeper

Ballast is the last resort, not the first. A nose-heavy aircraft can often be fixed by moving equipment aft (a battery relocation is a classic) rather than carrying dead weight — every pound of ballast comes straight out of the useful load for the life of the aircraft. When you do fit fixed ballast, it goes in the equipment list, in the amendment, and in a placard if it must never be removed.

Summary

Weight decides performance; balance decides stability and control; and it is illegal to fly outside the envelope. The AME provides the empty weight and empty CG, the pilot works out the loaded ones. Everything is measured from the datum (aft +, forward −; butt lines right +, left −): arm is a distance, moment is weight × arm, and because the moments on a stationary object sum to zero, CG = total moment ÷ total weight — never add the arms. Empty weight includes everything fixed and installed plus residual fuel and (since 1978) full oil; useful load is max gross minus empty. The report is a required record (CAR 605.92, Std 571 App C); large aircraft are reweighed every five years or after major work including paint. To weigh: configure per the manual, clean and dry, closed hangar, level on at least three scales using the TCDS levelling means, subtract tare, account for fuel and oil (6 / 6.7 / 7.5 / 8.35 lb/gal), and fill the table. Check the CG against the envelope — the limits can change with weight, and large aircraft use % MAC. After an equipment change, calculate an amendment from the previous report (removed = negative), log it before the next flight, supersede the old page and keep it. Addendums hold alternate configurations; ballast is placed by arm = moment ÷ weight; the equipment list records everything outside the type design.

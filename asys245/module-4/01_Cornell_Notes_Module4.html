Primary Flight Controls

Which three axes does an aircraft rotate around, which primary control works each one, and what counts as a secondary control?

Aileron · Elevator · Rudder
  • An aircraft rotates about three axes that all cross at the CG: longitudinal (roll), lateral (pitch), vertical (yaw). One primary control per axis.
  • Ailerons (outer trailing edge of each wing) → roll about the longitudinal axis. They move opposite ways: the down aileron adds lift on that wing, the up aileron reduces it — the wing with more lift rises.
  • Elevator (trailing edge of the horizontal stabilizer) → pitch about the lateral axis. Stick back = elevator up = nose up.
  • Rudder (trailing edge of the fin) → yaw about the vertical axis. Deflecting it makes a side force on the tail: right pedal = rudder right = nose right.
  • Every primary control works the same way: it changes the camber of the surface it is on → more or less lift on that side.
  • Secondary (auxiliary) controls = flaps, slats, slots, spoilers, speed brakes and trim tabs. They change lift, drag or control force; they do not fly the aircraft by themselves.

Go deeper

Think of every control surface as a small hinged flap that bends the airflow. Deflect it down and the surface it sits on makes more lift on that side; deflect it up and it makes less. Ailerons use this in opposite directions to roll; the elevator and rudder use it to push the tail up, down or sideways.

Stabilator & Anti-Servo Tab

What is a stabilator, and why does it need an anti-servo tab?

One-piece tail
  • A stabilator is a one-piece horizontal tail — the whole surface pivots instead of a fixed stabilizer plus a hinged elevator. (Piper Cherokee, many jets.)
  • It is very powerful, so the danger is over-controlling: small stick movement = big pitch change.
  • The anti-servo tab on its trailing edge moves the same direction as the stabilator. That adds feel — the pilot must push harder the further the stabilator moves.
  • The anti-servo tab usually doubles as the trim tab on these aircraft. Balance weights may also be fitted to the stabilator.
  • Compare a servo tab, which moves the opposite way to help move a heavy surface (big aircraft with no boost).
Go deeper

Here is the easy way to keep these two straight. A servo tab is a helper — it swings the opposite way to the surface so the air does some of the pushing for the pilot. An anti-servo tab is the opposite of a helper: it swings the same way, so the air pushes back and the pilot feels it. Why would you want a control that fights you? Because a stabilator is a huge, powerful surface. Without something pushing back it would feel like nothing in your hand, and a small twitch at cruise speed could pitch the aircraft hard enough to hurt it. The anti-servo tab gives the pilot a real, progressive feel — a little push for a little movement, a big push for a big one.

Trim Tabs

What does a trim tab do, and which way does it move compared with the control surface?

Hands-off flight
  • A trim tab is a small hinged piece on the trailing edge of a control surface. Its job: hold the surface in place so the pilot can fly hands-off.
  • It moves opposite to the direction you want the control surface to go. Tab up → airflow pushes the tab down → elevator goes down → nose-down trim. Tab down → elevator up → nose up.
  • Trim takes away the sustained control force in one flight condition (climb, cruise, approach) so the pilot is not holding pressure the whole time.
  • Adjustable trim is set from the cockpit: a manual trim wheel on small aircraft, an electric motor and switch on larger ones. Usually on the elevator; some aircraft also have aileron or rudder trim tabs.
  • Fixed trim tab = a small metal tab an AME bends on the ground to fix a wing-heavy or nose-heavy tendency. Test-fly, bend a little, test again.
  • Trim removes pilot effort; it does not add control authority.
Go deeper

A trim tab is a lever on a lever. The tab is tiny, but it sits at the very back of the control surface, so a small tab force makes a big turning moment on the surface. That is why a fixed tab only needs a few degrees of bend to cure a wing-heavy aircraft — bend it in small steps.

Balancing the Controls

Name three ways designers balance a control surface, and what each one is for.

Lighter · No flutter
  • Aerodynamic balance = part of the surface sits ahead of the hinge (a horn at the tip, or an overhang). Air hits that part and helps move the surface → lighter controls.
  • Balance tab = a tab linked so it moves opposite to the surface, using airflow to help the pilot. (A servo tab is the free-floating version.)
  • Balance panels (big aircraft ailerons) = a panel in a sealed bay ahead of the hinge; the pressure difference across it helps push the surface. Both tabs and panels exist for the same reason: heavy control loads.
  • Mass balance = a weight ahead of the hinge so the surface's CG sits on or in front of the hinge line. Purpose: stop flutter — a violent vibration that can tear the surface off.
  • AME rule: after repair or repaint, a control surface must be re-balanced to the manual's limits. Paint adds weight behind the hinge.
Go deeper

Flutter is the reason mass balance matters so much to maintenance. If a surface's centre of gravity is behind the hinge, a gust can start it flapping, and at speed the flapping feeds on itself. That is why the manual gives a balance limit in inch-pounds and why you weigh the surface on a balance beam after any repair or repaint.

Flaps

Name the four flap types from simplest to most effective, and what flaps do to lift and drag.

Plain · Split · Slotted · Fowler
  • Flaps are on the inner trailing edge. Lowering them increases camber → more lift and more drag → slower, steeper approach and shorter take-off.
  • Plain flap — simple hinge, whole trailing edge bends down. Cheapest, least effective.
  • Split flap — only the bottom surface hinges down. More drag than lift; good for steep approaches.
  • Slotted flap — a gap opens between wing and flap; high-pressure air from below flows over the flap and keeps the flow attached → more lift, later stall.
  • Fowler flap — slides back and down on tracks. Increases wing area and camber. Most lift, most complex (airliners use double- and triple-slotted Fowler flaps).
  • Rule of thumb: the first ~20° of flap gives mostly lift with little drag (take-off); beyond that, mostly drag (landing).
  • Landing: flaps let you fly a steeper approach without picking up speed, and keep control at a lower speed.
Go deeper

Why does the slot help? Air under the wing is at high pressure; air over the flap is slowing down and about to separate. The slot lets the high-pressure air squirt through onto the top of the flap, giving the boundary layer new energy so it stays attached to a much steeper angle. Fowler flaps add the slot and extra wing area — the best of both — which is why big jets use them.

Slots & Slats

What is the difference between a slot and a slat, and what do they do to the stall?

Leading edge
  • Both are leading-edge devices that let the wing reach a higher angle of attack before stalling.
  • Slot = a fixed gap built into the leading edge. High-pressure air from below flows through onto the top surface and keeps the flow attached. Simple, but adds drag all the time.
  • Slat = a movable piece of leading edge that slides forward (automatically by air pressure, or by the pilot/hydraulics) to open a slot only when needed.
  • Often placed near the wingtips so the tips (and ailerons) keep flying after the wing root stalls → roll control right into the stall.
  • Big jets: leading-edge slats + trailing-edge Fowler flaps = the full high-lift system.
Go deeper

An automatic slat is elegant: at low speed and high angle of attack the low pressure near the leading edge simply sucks the slat forward; at cruise speed the airflow pushes it closed. No motor, no pilot action — the STOL bush planes you see around Southern Alberta often use fixed slots for the same reason.

Spoilers & Speed Brakes

What do spoilers do, how are flight spoilers different from ground spoilers, and what is a speed brake?

Kill lift · Add drag
  • A spoiler is a panel on the top of the wing that rises into the airflow. It spoils lift and adds drag.
  • Flight spoilers — raise on one wing to help the ailerons roll (no adverse yaw), or on both wings to descend fast without speeding up.
  • Ground spoilers — all panels pop up on touchdown, automatically or from a lift sensor: dump the lift so the weight goes onto the wheels → brakes work on the rollout.
  • Speed brakes — panels whose only job is drag, to slow down in flight or on landing: on the upper and lower wing surfaces, the fuselage or the tail cone. They are placed and worked symmetrically so they do not add unwanted pitch, roll or yaw.
  • Gliders use spoilers to control the glide path — they have no engine to throttle back.
Go deeper

Spoilers solve the adverse-yaw problem from Module 3 neatly. An aileron makes roll by adding lift (and drag) on the rising wing; a spoiler makes roll by removing lift on the dropping wing, and its drag is on the inside of the turn — exactly where you want it. That is why fast jets use spoilers for roll at high speed.

Summary

Three primary controls — ailerons (roll), elevator (pitch), rudder (yaw) — all work by changing camber. A stabilator replaces stabilizer-plus-elevator and needs an anti-servo tab for feel; trim tabs move opposite to the surface so the pilot can fly hands-off; horn, balance-tab and balance-panel designs make controls lighter, and mass balance stops flutter. Flaps add camber (plain, split, slotted, Fowler — in order of effectiveness), slots and slats delay the stall at the leading edge, spoilers kill lift and add drag, and speed brakes add drag only.

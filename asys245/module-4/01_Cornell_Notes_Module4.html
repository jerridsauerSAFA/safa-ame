Primary Flight Controls

What are the three primary controls, what does each one move, and what counts as a secondary control?

Aileron · Elevator · Rudder
  • Ailerons (outer trailing edge of each wing) → roll about the longitudinal axis. They move opposite ways: one up, one down.
  • Elevator (trailing edge of the horizontal stabilizer) → pitch about the lateral axis. Stick back = elevator up = nose up.
  • Rudder (trailing edge of the fin) → yaw about the vertical axis. Left pedal = rudder left = nose left.
  • Every primary control works the same way: it changes the camber of the surface it is on → more or less lift on that side.
  • Secondary (auxiliary) controls = flaps, slats, slots, spoilers, speed brakes and trim tabs. They change lift, drag or control force; they do not fly the aircraft by themselves.
Go deeper

Think of every control surface as a small hinged flap that bends the airflow. Deflect it down and the surface it sits on makes more lift on that side; deflect it up and it makes less. Ailerons use this in opposite directions to roll; the elevator and rudder use it to push the tail up, down or sideways.

Stabilator & Anti-Servo Tab

What is a stabilator, and why does it need an anti-servo tab?

One-piece tail
  • A stabilator is a one-piece horizontal tail — the whole surface pivots instead of a fixed stabilizer plus a hinged elevator. (Piper Cherokee, many jets.)
  • It is very powerful, so the danger is over-controlling: small stick movement = big pitch change.
  • The anti-servo tab on its trailing edge moves the same direction as the stabilator. That adds feel — the pilot must push harder the further the stabilator moves.
  • The anti-servo tab usually doubles as the trim tab on these aircraft.
  • Compare a servo tab, which moves the opposite way to help move a heavy surface (big aircraft with no boost).
Go deeper

Why "anti-servo"? A servo tab is a helper: it moves opposite to the surface and uses the airflow to push the surface for the pilot. An anti-servo tab does the reverse — it resists the movement so the pilot gets a natural, progressive load. Without it a stabilator would feel dangerously light, especially at high speed.

Trim Tabs

What does a trim tab do, and which way does it move compared with the control surface?

Hands-off flight
  • A trim tab is a small hinged piece on the trailing edge of a control surface. Its job: hold the surface in place so the pilot can fly hands-off.
  • It moves opposite to the direction you want the control surface to go. Tab down → airflow pushes the tab up → the elevator goes up → nose up.
  • Adjustable trim is set from the cockpit (wheel, crank or electric switch) — usually on the elevator, sometimes rudder and ailerons.
  • Fixed trim tab = a small metal tab an AME bends on the ground to fix a wing-heavy or nose-heavy tendency. Test-fly, bend a little, test again.
  • Trim removes pilot effort; it does not add control authority.
Go deeper

A trim tab is a lever on a lever. The tab is tiny, but it sits at the very back of the control surface, so a small tab force makes a big turning moment on the surface. That is why a fixed tab only needs a few degrees of bend to cure a wing-heavy aircraft — bend it in small steps.

Balancing the Controls

Name three ways designers balance a control surface, and what each one is for.

Lighter · No flutter
  • Aerodynamic balance = part of the surface sits ahead of the hinge (a horn at the tip, or an overhang). Air hits that part and helps move the surface → lighter controls.
  • Balance tab = a tab linked so it moves opposite to the surface, using airflow to help the pilot. (A servo tab is the free-floating version.)
  • Balance panels (big aircraft ailerons) = a panel in a sealed bay ahead of the hinge; pressure difference across it helps push the surface.
  • Mass balance = a weight ahead of the hinge so the surface's CG sits on or in front of the hinge line. Purpose: stop flutter — a violent vibration that can tear the surface off.
  • AME rule: after repair or repaint, a control surface must be re-balanced to the manual's limits. Paint adds weight behind the hinge.
Go deeper

Flutter is the reason mass balance matters so much to maintenance. If a surface's centre of gravity is behind the hinge, a gust can start it flapping, and at speed the flapping feeds on itself. That is why the manual gives a balance limit in inch-pounds and why you weigh the surface on a balance beam after any repair or repaint.

Flaps

Name the four flap types from simplest to most effective, and what flaps do to lift and drag.

Plain · Split · Slotted · Fowler
  • Flaps are on the inner trailing edge. Lowering them increases camber → more lift and more drag → slower, steeper approach and shorter take-off.
  • Plain flap — simple hinge, whole trailing edge bends down. Cheapest, least effective.
  • Split flap — only the bottom surface hinges down. More drag than lift; good for steep approaches.
  • Slotted flap — a gap opens between wing and flap; high-pressure air from below flows over the flap and keeps the flow attached → more lift, later stall.
  • Fowler flap — slides back and down on tracks. Increases wing area and camber. Most lift, most complex (airliners use double- and triple-slotted Fowler flaps).
  • Rule of thumb: the first ~20° of flap gives mostly lift with little drag (take-off); beyond that, mostly drag (landing).
Go deeper

Why does the slot help? Air under the wing is at high pressure; air over the flap is slowing down and about to separate. The slot lets the high-pressure air squirt through onto the top of the flap, giving the boundary layer new energy so it stays attached to a much steeper angle. Fowler flaps add the slot and extra wing area — the best of both — which is why big jets use them.

Slots & Slats

What is the difference between a slot and a slat, and what do they do to the stall?

Leading edge
  • Both are leading-edge devices that let the wing reach a higher angle of attack before stalling.
  • Slot = a fixed gap built into the leading edge. High-pressure air from below flows through onto the top surface and keeps the flow attached. Simple, but adds drag all the time.
  • Slat = a movable piece of leading edge that slides forward (automatically by air pressure, or by the pilot/hydraulics) to open a slot only when needed.
  • Often placed near the wingtips so the tips (and ailerons) keep flying after the wing root stalls → roll control right into the stall.
  • Big jets: leading-edge slats + trailing-edge Fowler flaps = the full high-lift system.
Go deeper

An automatic slat is elegant: at low speed and high angle of attack the low pressure near the leading edge simply sucks the slat forward; at cruise speed the airflow pushes it closed. No motor, no pilot action — the STOL bush planes you see around Southern Alberta often use fixed slots for the same reason.

Spoilers & Speed Brakes

What do spoilers do, how are flight spoilers different from ground spoilers, and what is a speed brake?

Kill lift · Add drag
  • A spoiler is a panel on the top of the wing that rises into the airflow. It spoils lift and adds drag.
  • Flight spoilers — raise on one wing to help the ailerons roll (no adverse yaw), or on both wings to descend fast without speeding up.
  • Ground spoilers — all panels pop up on touchdown: dump the lift so the weight goes onto the wheels → brakes work.
  • Speed brakes — panels whose only job is drag: on the fuselage (jets) or tail cone. They are balanced (symmetrical) so they do not roll or pitch the aircraft.
  • Gliders use spoilers to control the glide path — they have no engine to throttle back.
Go deeper

Spoilers solve the adverse-yaw problem from Module 3 neatly. An aileron makes roll by adding lift (and drag) on the rising wing; a spoiler makes roll by removing lift on the dropping wing, and its drag is on the inside of the turn — exactly where you want it. That is why fast jets use spoilers for roll at high speed.

Control System Types

Name the four ways the cockpit can be connected to the control surfaces, from simplest to newest.

Cable → Rod → Hydraulic → Wire
  • Cable systems — steel cables over pulleys. Light, cheap, easy to route around corners. Most light aircraft. Needs correct tension.
  • Push-pull rod systems — rigid tubes with rod ends; no stretch, no tension checks, but heavier and must run fairly straight. Often mixed with cables (Cessna aileron rods, Piper).
  • Hydraulically assisted / powered — pilot moves a valve; hydraulic actuators move the surface. Needed when air loads are too high for muscles (big jets). Artificial feel gives the pilot resistance.
  • Fly-by-wire — no mechanical link. Pilot input → computer → electrical signal → actuator. Airbus, Boeing 777/787, most military jets.
  • All of them still end at the same thing: a hinge and a surface that changes camber.
Go deeper

Most aircraft you will work on in this program are cable or cable-plus-rod. The maintenance skills that go with them — tensiometers, turnbuckle safety, rigging pins — are exactly the hands-on items in the AME practical exams, so the next three sections are the ones to know cold.

Cable System Hardware

What do pulleys, fairleads, turnbuckles, bellcranks and torque tubes each do — and what are the two numbers to remember?

3° · 3 threads
  • Pulleys change the cable's direction. A guard stops the cable jumping off when slack.
  • Fairleads (plastic or phenolic guides) keep a cable from rubbing on structure. A fairlead may bend the cable no more than 3° — more than that needs a pulley.
  • Turnbuckles adjust cable tension. Safe when no more than 3 threads show outside the barrel; always safetied (wire or clip) after adjustment.
  • Bellcranks change the direction and can change the ratio of the motion (lever). Quadrants turn cable pull into rotation.
  • Torque tubes carry the motion as a twist — e.g. from the control column to the ailerons.
  • Cable tension is measured with a tensiometer and corrected for temperature using the manual's chart — cables tighten when cold, slacken when hot.
  • Stops limit travel: primary stops at the surface, secondary stops at the cockpit control.
Go deeper

The two numbers are exam favourites: 3° for a fairlead, 3 threads for a turnbuckle. Both come from the same idea — a cable is happy being pulled straight and unhappy being bent or held by too little thread. Cable tension itself varies by aircraft (a typical light aircraft is in the tens of pounds), so always use the type's chart, never a remembered number.

Push-Pull Rods & Rod Ends

How is a rod end adjusted and locked, and what is the check hole for?

Jam nut · Check hole
  • A push-pull rod is a tube with a threaded rod end (clevis or ball-bearing end) at each end. Screw the rod end in or out to set the length.
  • A jam nut is tightened against the rod end to lock the adjustment. Loose jam nut = length can change in flight = rigging drifts.
  • The check hole (inspection hole) in the tube shows thread engagement: if a wire or pin cannot pass through the hole, enough thread is screwed in. If it can, the rod end is not engaged far enough — unsafe.
  • Rod ends with spherical bearings allow small misalignment without binding.
  • Rods must be inspected for bends, corrosion inside and out (drain holes), and bearing play.
Go deeper

The check hole is a one-second safety test that catches a very serious fault. A rod end held by only two or three threads can pull out under load and leave a control surface disconnected. On some rods a slot or witness mark does the same job — the manual for the type tells you which.

Fly-by-Wire, Rigging & Control Checks

What does fly-by-wire add, what does "rigging" mean, and why do control-system jobs need a second signature?

CAR 571.10
  • Fly-by-wire benefits: lighter (no cables/rods), envelope protection (the computer will not let the pilot stall, over-bank or over-stress the aircraft), smoother gust response, easier autopilot integration.
  • Risk: everything depends on electrical power and software → multiple redundant computers and power sources; a direct law backup mode when protections are lost.
  • Rigging = setting the control system so every surface sits in the right neutral position and moves the right amount — using rigging pins, protractors, cable tension charts and the manual's travel limits.
  • Control check after any work: move each control full travel and confirm the surface moves the correct direction, full travel, freely, and reaches the stops. Reversed controls have crashed aircraft.
  • In Canada, work on flight controls requires an independent (duplicate) inspection by a second qualified person before release — CAR 571.10. Two signatures, no exceptions.
Go deeper

Envelope protection is the big idea behind fly-by-wire: the aircraft's limits are written into the software, so a startled pilot can pull full back on the stick and the aircraft gives maximum performance without stalling. The trade-off is that the AME now troubleshoots sensors, computers and wiring rather than pulleys. The duplicate-inspection rule exists because a single reversed cable is invisible from the cockpit and fatal on take-off.

Summary

Three primary controls — ailerons (roll), elevator (pitch), rudder (yaw) — all work by changing camber. A stabilator replaces stabilizer-plus-elevator and needs an anti-servo tab for feel; trim tabs move opposite to the surface so the pilot can fly hands-off; horn, balance-tab and balance-panel designs make controls lighter, and mass balance stops flutter. Flaps add camber (plain, split, slotted, Fowler — in order of effectiveness), slots and slats delay the stall at the leading edge, spoilers kill lift and add drag, and speed brakes add drag only. Cockpit to surface is connected by cables (pulleys, fairleads ≤ 3°, turnbuckles ≤ 3 threads, tensiometer), push-pull rods (jam nut, check hole), hydraulic power with artificial feel, or fly-by-wire with envelope protection. After any control work: rig it, check direction and travel, and get the independent inspection required by CAR 571.10.

Three Phases & the Drag Curve

What are the three phases of flight, and what happens to parasite, induced and total drag as the aircraft speeds up?

Climb · Cruise · Descent
  • Every flight has three phases: climb (including take-off), cruise (level flight) and descent (including landing). Performance = how well the aircraft does each one.
  • Parasite drag (skin friction, form, interference) rises with speed — roughly with the square of it. Faster = much more parasite drag.
  • Induced drag (the price of making lift) falls as speed rises. This is the part that feels backwards: slow flight needs a high angle of attack, and that makes lots of induced drag.
  • Total drag = parasite + induced. Plot it against speed and you get a U-shaped curve.
  • The bottom of the U is where parasite and induced drag are equal. That speed is L/DMAX — the least drag for the lift you need, and the most efficient speed to fly. It matters in every phase, especially cruise and glide.

Slow: induced drag dominates. Fast: parasite drag dominates. Where they cross, total drag is lowest = L/D max.

Go deeper

Why does induced drag fall with speed? Lift has to equal weight. At low speed the only way to get that lift is a big angle of attack, which tilts the lift vector back and spills strong wingtip vortices — both cost drag. Speed up and the wing can make the same lift at a small angle, so induced drag shrinks. Parasite drag has no such help: more air hitting the airframe per second means more friction and pressure drag, roughly four times for double the speed.

Climb: Best Angle vs Best Rate

What is the difference between Vx and Vy, and when would a pilot use each one?

Vx · Vy
  • Two ways to measure a climb: how much height you gain per distance, and how much per time.
  • Best angle of climb — Vx: the most height over the ground distance covered. The steepest climb. Use it to clear an obstacle (trees, a fence) right after take-off.
  • Best rate of climb — Vy: the most height in a given time. The quickest way to altitude. Use it for a normal climb once obstacles are behind you.
  • Vx is a slower airspeed than Vy: the nose is higher, so the path is steeper but the climb takes longer.
  • Both speeds come from the POH for that aircraft; they change with weight and altitude.

Vx: steepest path (clears the obstacle). Vy: fastest climb by the clock — a shallower path, but you reach altitude sooner.

Go deeper

Think of climbing a hill. Vx is scrambling straight up the steep face — you gain the most height for every step forward, but it is slow going. Vy is the switchback trail — you cover more ground, but you reach the top sooner. Vx and Vy get closer together as the aircraft climbs, and they meet at the absolute ceiling, where the aircraft can no longer climb at all.

Cruise: Power, Altitude & Fuel

In cruise, what decides the aircraft's speed, and why does climbing higher give more speed for the same fuel?

Power setting · Altitude
  • Airspeed is measured in knots (nautical miles per hour). Knots × 1.852 = km/h. Example: 250 kt = 463 km/h.
  • In cruise, fuel burn is proportional to brake horsepower. Run the engine at 75% power and you burn about 20% more fuel than at 55%.
  • Cruise speed depends on two things: the power setting and the altitude.
  • Higher altitude = thinner air = less drag, so the same power pushes the aircraft faster. From the deck's chart: at 75% power the aircraft does 140 kt at sea level and 150 kt at 8,000 ft — 10 kt faster for the same fuel burn.
  • Same idea at 65% power: climb from 2,000 ft to 8,000 ft (standard day) and you go about 9 kt faster for no extra fuel.
  • The catch: the engine also loses power as the air thins, so there is a best cruise altitude for each aircraft — the POH chart tells you.

Same 75% power, same fuel: 10 kt faster at 8,000 ft because thinner air means less drag.

Go deeper

Fuel flow follows horsepower because the engine burns a roughly fixed amount of fuel for each horsepower-hour it produces. Altitude does not change that; what it changes is drag. Thinner air means fewer air molecules hitting the airframe each second, so the same thrust pushes the aircraft faster. Jets exploit this far more than piston aircraft — that is why airliners cruise at 35,000 ft and above.

The Power Curve & Descent

What makes an aircraft descend, and what decides how fast it sinks?

Available vs Required
  • Power required = the power it takes to hold level flight at each airspeed (it follows the drag curve: high at slow speed, lowest near L/D max, high again at speed).
  • Power available = what the engine and propeller can actually deliver at that airspeed and altitude.
  • Available more than required → the extra power goes into a climb. Available equal to required → level flight. Available less than required → a descent.
  • The rate of sink (or rate of climb) is proportional to the gap between the two curves. Pull the power back a little = a gentle descent; pull it back a lot = a steep one.
  • Throttle to idle and the power-available curve drops to zero (it sits on the x-axis). Power required stays exactly the same — the whole gap is now sink.
  • The lowest rate of sink is at the airspeed where power required is lowest — the bottom of the power-required curve.

Gap with available on top = climb. Gap with required on top = descent. Bigger gap = faster climb or sink.

Go deeper

This is the same picture as the drag curve, just multiplied by speed (power = drag × velocity). The point where the two curves meet on the right is the aircraft's maximum level-flight speed. Where they meet on the left — at low speed — is the slowest it can hold altitude with full power. Everything a pilot does with the throttle is moving along, or between, these two lines.

Region of Reversed Command

What is the "back of the power curve", and why is it dangerous?

Slower needs MORE power
  • Normal flying (the front of the power curve): slow down and you need less power; speed up and you need more. Throttle and speed go together.
  • Below the speed for minimum power required you enter the region of reversed command, also called the back of the power curve. Here the rule flips: slow down and you need MORE power.
  • Why: at low speed, any decrease in airspeed means a higher angle of attack and a jump in induced drag — so total drag goes up. Any increase in airspeed lowers total drag.
  • The danger is on a slow, dragged-in approach: the aircraft sinks, the pilot pulls the nose up, speed drops, drag rises, it sinks faster. The fix is counter-intuitive — add power and lower the nose.
  • The region is entered only at low airspeed; keep approach speeds at or above the POH numbers and you stay out of it.

Left of the minimum: slowing down raises drag, so you need more power to hold altitude — the "back side".

Go deeper

Pilots call it "behind the power curve" because the aircraft is behind where the throttle can rescue it. Picture a short-field approach flown too slowly: the aircraft is mushing along at a high angle of attack with lots of power just to stay level. If it sinks, there may be no extra power left — and pulling back only makes it worse. Bush pilots and carrier pilots live in this region on purpose, but with precise speed control and the engine's response already in hand.

Glide & Range: Best L/D

What is glide ratio, which speed gives the longest glide, and why can't you extend range just by carrying more fuel?

Glide ratio · L/D
  • Glide ratio = horizontal distance travelled ÷ height lost. A 10:1 glide ratio means 10 m forward for every 1 m down.
  • The lift-to-drag ratio (L/D) is the same number another way. In a glide, lift is almost equal to weight and stays essentially constant for a given weight — so L/D only changes when drag changes. Least drag = best L/D = longest glide.
  • Best glide speed (at L/D max) gives the most distance. Minimum sink speed (a bit slower) gives the most time in the air. Want to reach a field? Fly best glide — slightly faster than minimum sink.
  • Maximum glide distance is at the lowest ratio of sink rate to forward speed.
  • Range = the distance an aircraft can fly on its fuel. The speed for maximum range is at best L/D too — usually well below normal cruise speed. Set the throttle to the power required for best L/D.
  • You cannot extend range just by adding fuel: fuel adds weight, and weight makes performance worse (more drag to carry it).

Glide ratio = distance ÷ height. Best glide speed (L/D max) gives the longest distance; minimum sink gives the longest time.

Go deeper

The Gimli Glider (1983): an Air Canada 767 ran out of fuel at 41,000 ft over Manitoba after a unit-conversion mistake in fuelling. The crew flew it at best glide — a 767 manages roughly 12:1 — and dead-sticked it onto a closed runway at Gimli. Nobody died. The same L/D arithmetic on this page is what got them there, and the fuelling error is a reminder that the AME's numbers on the ground decide what the aircraft can do in the air.

Airspeed & the V-Speeds

Why does a pilot need to know the airspeed, and how is airspeed controlled with and without engine power?

Vso · Vfe · Va · Vne · Vx · Vy
  • Airspeed = the speed of the aircraft through the air (not over the ground). Measured in knots.
  • The pilot needs it because every important limit is a speed — the V-speeds:
  • Vso stall speed in the landing configuration (flaps and gear down) · Vfe maximum speed with flaps extended · Va manoeuvring speed (full control deflection is safe at or below it) · Vne never-exceed speed (the red line) · Vx / Vy best angle / best rate of climb (and the matching descent speeds).
  • Airspeed also feeds navigation: it is what you use to work out ETE (estimated time en route) and ETA (estimated time of arrival).
  • With power, in straight and level flight, the pilot sets airspeed with the engine power / RPM.
  • With no power (engine failure, or a glide), the pilot controls airspeed with attitude — the angle of the nose relative to the horizon. Nose down = faster; nose up = slower.

The V-speeds are the limits a pilot flies by; an AME sees them as the markings on the airspeed indicator.

Go deeper

Why can a glider — or an aircraft with a dead engine — still control its speed? Because gravity is now the engine. Lower the nose and the aircraft trades height for speed; raise it and speed bleeds off. That is why the first rule after an engine failure is "fly the aircraft": set the glide attitude for best glide speed before anything else. For the AME, Va matters at the hangar too — it is the speed the structure is certified to take full control deflection at.

The Boundary Layer

What is the boundary layer, and how does it give a wing an "effective shape" that is different from its real shape?

Sticky air
  • Air molecules right against a surface stick to it — their speed is zero. The molecules just above are slowed by bumping into the stuck ones, and they slow the layer above them, and so on.
  • The farther from the surface, the less the surface matters. The result is a thin layer where the air speed goes from zero at the surface up to the free-stream speed away from it. That layer is the boundary layer.
  • The airflow outside reacts to the edge of the boundary layer as if it were the surface. So every object has an effective shape that is slightly different from (slightly fatter than) its physical shape.
  • If the boundary layer separates (lifts off the surface), the effective shape becomes very different from the real one — and the wing stops working like a wing.
  • Keeping the boundary layer attached is what slots, slats, slotted flaps and vortex generators are all about (Module 4).

Inside the boundary layer the speed climbs from zero at the skin to full speed at the edge. The outside flow "sees" that edge as the wing.

Go deeper

The boundary layer is why surface condition matters so much to an AME. Frost, dents, bad rivet lines or a rough paint repair thicken the layer and make it separate sooner — the wing stalls earlier and at a higher speed than the book says. On a small wing the boundary layer is only millimetres thick, which is why a strip of frost the thickness of sandpaper is enough to change the stall.

Stall & Stall Patterns

What causes a wing to stall, and why do designers want the stall to start at the wing root?

Root first
  • For a thin airfoil, lift is directly proportional to angle of attack at small angles (roughly +10° to −10°). More angle, more lift — in a straight line.
  • Past that, the boundary layer separates from the top of the wing. Lift drops abruptly and drag jumps. That is a stall. A stall is about angle of attack, not speed — the wing can stall at any speed if the angle is high enough.
  • Different wing shapes stall in different patterns: a rectangular wing tends to stall at the root first; tapered and swept wings tend to stall at the tips first.
  • The desirable pattern is root first: the tips, where the ailerons are, keep flying, so the pilot keeps roll control at high angles of attack and gets a warning (buffet) before the whole wing lets go.
  • A tip-first stall is nasty: one tip usually goes before the other, the aircraft rolls, and the ailerons are already in stalled air. Designers fix it with washout (tip set at a lower angle), stall strips at the root, or tip slots/slats.
  • Stall recovery = reduce the angle of attack: nose down, then power, then level the wings.

Designers want the stall to start at the root so the ailerons keep working and the pilot gets a warning.

Go deeper

Why does a rectangular wing stall at the root? Its tip vortex spills around the tip and actually reduces the local angle of attack out there, so the tip is the last part to reach the critical angle. A tapered wing has a weaker tip vortex and a higher local lift coefficient near the tip, so the tip goes first. A stall strip is a small triangular strip on the root leading edge that trips the flow early on purpose — an AME replaces it in exactly the spot the manual shows, because moving it changes the stall behaviour.

Vortex Generators

What is a vortex generator, and how does a tiny fin delay the stall?

Energize the layer
  • Vortex generators (VGs) are small fins in a row along the top of the wing (sometimes on the tail too).
  • As air flows back across the wing it loses energy in the boundary layer. As it loses energy it starts to separate — and separation is a stall.
  • Each VG spins off a small vortex. The vortex mixes the fast, high-energy air from outside the boundary layer down into it, re-energizing the slow air near the skin.
  • Result: the boundary layer stays attached to a higher angle of attack → the wing is less likely to stall, the stall speed drops, and the controls keep working longer.
  • Common as a bolt-on STOL kit on bush aircraft; also used ahead of ailerons and on the fin. The cost is a small amount of extra drag in cruise.

A small fin spins off a vortex that stirs fast air into the tired boundary layer, so it stays attached longer.

Go deeper

Look at the top of a Cessna 180 or a Bonanza with a STOL kit: a neat row of little aluminum tabs about a third of the way back from the leading edge. Each one is only a few centimetres tall — about the thickness of the boundary layer — and that is the point. It only needs to reach the fast air just outside the layer to drag some of it down. For the AME, VG kits come with an STC: position, spacing and count are fixed by the approval, and a missing VG is a snag, not a cosmetic fault.

Mach Number & the Speed of Sound

What is Mach number, what is critical Mach number, and why does the speed of sound change with altitude?

M = V ÷ a
  • Mach number = the aircraft's speed divided by the speed of sound. Mach 0.5 = half the speed of sound; Mach 2 = twice it.
  • The "speed of sound" is really the speed at which a small disturbance (pressure wave) travels through the air. Sound itself is just what the brain makes of those waves.
  • In the standard atmosphere at sea level it is about 761 mph (about 661 knots). It depends on air temperature — colder air = slower speed of sound. So at high altitude, where it is cold, an aircraft reaches a given Mach number at a lower true airspeed.
  • Critical Mach number (Mcrit) = the aircraft speed at which the air flowing over the wing first reaches Mach 1. The air over the top of a wing is faster than the aircraft, so the wing goes supersonic locally before the aircraft does.
  • Below Mcrit every bit of flow over the surface is subsonic. At Mcrit the fastest spot on the top surface just touches Mach 1.
  • History: 14 October 1947 — Capt. Chuck Yeager flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 "Glamorous Glennis" past Mach 1, air-launched from a B-29. Bullet-shaped fuselage, thin strong wings, and an adjustable horizontal stabilizer made it possible.

The aircraft can still be well below Mach 1 (about 0.8) when the air over its wing first hits Mach 1 — that is critical Mach.

Go deeper

Why temperature and not altitude? A pressure wave moves by molecules bumping their neighbours, and warm molecules move faster, so the wave travels faster. Cold air slows it. At 35,000 ft on a standard day the speed of sound is only about 575 kt, against about 661 kt at sea level — so an airliner cruising at Mach 0.82 is doing roughly 470 kt true. That is also why the X-1 was flown high: colder air meant Mach 1 arrived at a lower, safer true airspeed.

Shock Waves, Transonic Flight & Shock Stall

What is a shock wave, what changes as air passes through one, and what happens to drag in the transonic range?

Wave drag
  • A shock wave is a very thin wall in the air where the flow jumps from supersonic to subsonic. A normal shock wave stands at right angles (perpendicular) to the surface.
  • As air passes through a shock wave: pressure increases, temperature increases, velocity decreases (and density increases — the air is suddenly squeezed).
  • Shock waves form in stages: first on the top surface only (A), then also on the bottom (B), and finally the whole airfoil is supersonic with a bow wave ahead of it (C). The pressure jump reaching the ground is the sonic boom.
  • Transonic flight = the transition from subsonic to supersonic, from about critical Mach (≈ 0.8) to about Mach 1.3. Some of the flow is subsonic and some supersonic at the same time.
  • Drag increases greatly in the transonic range. Energy is dumped into the air as heat and pressure jumps, and the engine has to make up for it. This extra drag is called wave drag. The Mach number where the drag rise really begins is the critical drag Mach (Mcrd).
  • Shock stall = the shock wave separates the boundary layer behind it. Lift is lost and buffet starts, even though the angle of attack is low. A stall caused by speed, not by angle.

Shock waves (orange) appear on top first, then below, then a bow wave ahead once the whole airfoil is supersonic.

Go deeper

Think of the shock wave as a traffic jam. Supersonic air cannot "hear" the wing coming — pressure information travels at the speed of sound, and the air is moving faster than that — so it piles into the obstacle without warning and slams to a slower speed in a wall a few molecules thick. Behind that wall the air is hotter, denser and slower, and that sudden pressure jump pushes the boundary layer off the surface. Early jets that got too fast hit shock stall and "Mach tuck": the shock moved aft, the centre of pressure moved with it, and the nose dropped into a dive the elevator could not fix.

Wings for High-Speed Flight

Name two wing design features that suit transonic and supersonic flight, and say what each one does.

Sweep · Thin
  • Sweepback: angling the wing back reduces the effective Mach number along the chord. Only the part of the airflow that runs straight across the wing (chordwise) counts for shock formation; the spanwise part is "wasted". A swept wing can therefore fly faster before reaching critical Mach.
  • Thin wings with sharp leading edges: a blunt nose pushes a bow wave out ahead of it (lots of drag). A sharp leading edge lets the bow wave attach to the leading edge and become an oblique shock wave.
  • When the shocks are oblique (slanted), all the air around the wing stays supersonic and smooth — far less wave drag than a detached normal shock.
  • Example: the Lockheed F-104 Starfighter — a high-altitude interceptor with tiny, razor-thin, sharp-edged wings; the first US jet fighter to fly at twice the speed of sound. NASA used it as a chase plane.
  • The trade-off: thin, swept wings make poor lift at low speed, so these aircraft need high landing speeds, big flaps or blown flaps.

Sweep lowers the Mach number the wing "feels"; a sharp, thin leading edge turns the bow wave into an attached oblique shock.

Go deeper

Sweep is a trick of geometry. If a wing is swept 35°, the airflow crossing the chord is the free stream multiplied by cos 35° — about 82%. So the wing "feels" Mach 0.82 when the aircraft is at Mach 1.0. That is why nearly every airliner has 25–35° of sweep: it buys cruise speed without the brutal low-speed handling of a true supersonic wing. The F-104 went the other way — almost no sweep, but a wing so thin and sharp that ground crews fitted guards on the leading edges.

Summary

Flight has three phases — climb, cruise, descent — and all of them live on the drag curve: parasite drag rises with speed, induced drag falls, and the bottom of the total-drag U is L/D max, the most efficient speed. Climb at Vx for angle (clear the obstacle) or Vy for rate (reach altitude fastest). In cruise, fuel burn follows horsepower and higher altitude buys speed for free because thinner air means less drag. Descent and sink rate come from the gap between power required and power available; below the minimum-power speed you are in the region of reversed command, where slowing down needs more power. Glide ratio and L/D are the same idea — best glide speed for distance, minimum sink for time — and range is also found at best L/D, not by carrying more fuel. Airspeed is controlled by power when you have it and by attitude when you don't, and the V-speeds are the limits. Air sticks to a surface and forms a boundary layer; when it separates the wing stalls, ideally root first so the ailerons keep working; vortex generators delay separation. Mach number is speed divided by the speed of sound, which depends on temperature; at critical Mach the flow over the wing first hits Mach 1, shock waves appear (pressure and temperature up, velocity down), wave drag rises sharply through the transonic range, and shock stall can follow. Sweepback and thin sharp wings are the designer's answers.

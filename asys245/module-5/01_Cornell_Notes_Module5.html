Three Phases & the Drag Curve

What are the three phases of flight, and what happens to parasite, induced and total drag as the aircraft speeds up?

Climb · Cruise · Descent
  • Every flight has three phases: climb (including take-off), cruise (level flight) and descent (including landing). Performance = how well the aircraft does each one.
  • Parasite drag (skin friction, form, interference) rises with speed — roughly with the square of it. Faster = much more parasite drag.
  • Induced drag (the price of making lift) falls as speed rises. This is the part that feels backwards: slow flight needs a high angle of attack, and that makes lots of induced drag.
  • Total drag = parasite + induced. Plot it against speed and you get a U-shaped curve.
  • The bottom of the U is where parasite and induced drag are equal. That speed is L/DMAX — the least drag for the lift you need, and the most efficient speed to fly. It matters in every phase, especially cruise and glide.

Slow: induced drag dominates. Fast: parasite drag dominates. Where they cross, total drag is lowest = L/D max.

Go deeper

Why does induced drag fall with speed? Lift has to equal weight. At low speed the only way to get that lift is a big angle of attack, which tilts the lift vector back and spills strong wingtip vortices — both cost drag. Speed up and the wing can make the same lift at a small angle, so induced drag shrinks. Parasite drag has no such help: more air hitting the airframe per second means more friction and pressure drag, roughly four times for double the speed.

Climb: Best Angle vs Best Rate

What is the difference between Vx and Vy, and when would a pilot use each one?

Vx · Vy
  • Two definitions we use for climb performance are: height gained per distance, and height gained per time.
  • Best angle of climb — Vx: the most height over the ground distance covered. The steepest climb. Use it to clear an obstacle (trees, a fence) right after take-off.
  • Best rate of climb — Vy: the most height in a given time. The quickest way to altitude. Use it for a normal climb once obstacles are behind you.
  • Vx is a slower airspeed than Vy: the nose is higher, so the path is steeper but the climb takes longer.

Vx: steepest path (clears the obstacle). Vy: fastest climb by the clock — a shallower path, but you reach altitude sooner.

Go deeper

Think of climbing a hill. Vx is scrambling straight up the steep face — you gain the most height for every step forward, but it is slow going. Vy is the switchback trail — you cover more ground, but you reach the top sooner. Vx and Vy get closer together as the aircraft climbs, and they meet at the absolute ceiling, where the aircraft can no longer climb at all.

Cruise: Power, Altitude & Fuel

In cruise, what decides the aircraft's speed, and why does climbing higher give more speed for the same fuel?

Power setting · Altitude
  • Airspeed can be measured in the following units: knots (kt — nautical miles per hour), miles per hour (mph) and kilometres per hour (km/h). 1 kt = 1.15 mph = 1.852 km/h. Example: 250 kt = 288 mph = 463 km/h.
  • In cruise, fuel burn is proportional to brake horsepower. Run the engine at 75% power and you burn about 20% more fuel than at 55%.
  • Cruise speed depends on two things: the power setting and the altitude.
  • Higher altitude = thinner air = less drag, so the same power pushes the aircraft faster. From the deck's chart: at 75% power the aircraft does 140 kt at sea level and 150 kt at 8,000 ft — 10 kt faster for the same fuel burn.
  • Same idea at 65% power: climb from 2,000 ft to 8,000 ft (standard day) and you go about 9 kt faster for no extra fuel.
  • The catch: the engine also loses power as the air thins, so there is a best cruise altitude for each aircraft — the POH chart tells you.

Same 75% power, same fuel: 10 kt faster at 8,000 ft because thinner air means less drag.

Go deeper

Fuel flow follows horsepower because the engine burns a roughly fixed amount of fuel for each horsepower-hour it produces. Altitude does not change that; what it changes is drag. Thinner air means fewer air molecules hitting the airframe each second, so the same thrust pushes the aircraft faster. Jets exploit this far more than piston aircraft — that is why airliners cruise at 35,000 ft and above.

The Power Curve & Descent

What makes an aircraft descend, and what decides how fast it sinks?

Available vs Required
  • Power required = the power it takes to hold level flight at each airspeed (it follows the drag curve: high at slow speed, lowest near L/D max, high again at speed).
  • Power available = what the engine and propeller can actually deliver at that airspeed and altitude.
  • Available more than required → the extra power goes into a climb. Available equal to required → level flight. Available less than required → a descent.
  • The rate of sink (or rate of climb) is proportional to the gap between the two curves. Pull the power back = a gentle sink (or descent); pull it back a lot = a steep one.
  • Throttle to idle and the power-available curve drops to zero (it sits on the x-axis). Power required stays exactly the same — the whole gap is now sink.
  • The lowest rate of sink is at the airspeed where power required is lowest — the bottom of the power-required curve.

Gap with available on top = climb. Gap with required on top = descent. Bigger gap = faster climb or sink.

Go deeper

This is the same picture as the drag curve, just multiplied by speed (power = drag × velocity). The point where the two curves meet on the right is the aircraft's maximum level-flight speed. Where they meet on the left — at low speed — is the slowest it can hold altitude with full power. Everything a pilot does with the throttle is moving along, or between, these two lines.

Glide & Range: Best L/D

What is glide ratio, which speed gives the longest glide, and why can't you extend range just by carrying more fuel?

Glide ratio · L/D
  • Glide ratio — the ratio of horizontal distance to vertical distance. A 10:1 glide ratio means 10 m forward for every 1 m down.
  • The lift-to-drag ratio (L/D) is the same number another way. In a glide, lift is almost equal to weight and stays essentially constant for a given weight — so L/D only changes when drag changes. Least drag = best L/D = longest glide.
  • Best glide speed (at L/D max) gives the most distance. Minimum sink speed (a bit slower) gives the most time in the air. Want to reach a field? Fly best glide — slightly faster than minimum sink.
  • Maximum glide distance is at the lowest ratio of sink rate to forward speed.
  • Range = the distance an aircraft can fly on its fuel. The speed for maximum range is at best L/D too — usually well below normal cruise speed. Set the throttle to the power required for best L/D.
  • You cannot extend range just by adding fuel: fuel adds weight, and weight makes performance worse (more drag to carry it).

Glide ratio = horizontal distance to vertical distance. Best glide speed (L/D max) gives the longest distance; minimum sink gives the longest time.

Go deeper

The Gimli Glider (1983): an Air Canada 767 ran out of fuel at 41,000 ft over Manitoba after a unit-conversion mistake in fuelling. The crew flew it at best glide — a 767 manages roughly 12:1 — and dead-sticked it onto a closed runway at Gimli. Nobody died. The same L/D arithmetic on this page is what got them there, and the fuelling error is a reminder that the AME's numbers on the ground decide what the aircraft can do in the air.

Airspeed & the V-Speeds

Why does a pilot need to know the airspeed, and how is airspeed controlled with and without engine power?

Vso · Vfe · Va · Vne · Vx · Vy
  • Airspeed = the speed of the aircraft through the air (not over the ground).
  • The pilot needs it because every important limit is a speed — the V-speeds:
  •   Vso — stall speed in the landing configuration (flaps and gear down).
  •   Vfe — maximum speed with the flaps extended.
  •   Va — manoeuvring speed; full control deflection is safe at or below it.
  •   Vne — never-exceed speed (the red line).
  •   Vx — best angle of climb.
  •   Vy — best rate of climb.
  • Airspeed also feeds navigation: it is what you use to work out ETE (estimated time en route) and ETA (estimated time of arrival).
  • With power, in straight and level flight, the pilot sets airspeed with the engine power / RPM.
  • With no power (engine failure, or a glide), the pilot controls airspeed with attitude — the angle of the nose relative to the horizon. Nose down = faster; nose up = slower.

The V-speeds are the limits a pilot flies by.

Go deeper

Why can a glider — or an aircraft with a dead engine — still control its speed? Because gravity is now the engine. Lower the nose and the aircraft trades height for speed; raise it and speed bleeds off. That is why the first rule after an engine failure is "fly the aircraft": set the glide attitude for best glide speed before anything else. For the AME, Va matters at the hangar too — it is the speed the structure is certified to take full control deflection at.

Summary

Flight has three phases — climb, cruise, descent — and all of them live on the drag curve: parasite drag rises with speed, induced drag falls, and the bottom of the total-drag U is L/D max, the most efficient speed. Climb at Vx for angle (clear the obstacle) or Vy for rate (reach altitude fastest). In cruise, fuel burn follows horsepower and higher altitude buys speed for free because thinner air means less drag. Descent and sink rate come from the gap between power required and power available. Glide ratio — horizontal distance to vertical distance — and L/D are the same idea — best glide speed for distance, minimum sink for time — and range is also found at best L/D, not by carrying more fuel. Airspeed is controlled by power when you have it and by attitude when you don't, and the V-speeds are the limits.

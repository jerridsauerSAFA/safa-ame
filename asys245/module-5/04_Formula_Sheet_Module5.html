Controlling airspeed: with power in level flight → throttle / RPM. With no power → attitude (nose angle to the horizon). Airspeed is also what you use for ETE and ETA.

Stall: boundary layer, patterns, fixes

IdeaWhat it isWhy it matters
Boundary layerThin layer where air speed rises from zero at the skin to free-stream at the edgeThe outside flow "sees" its edge as the wing (effective shape)
SeparationThe layer lifts off the surface at high angle of attackLift drops abruptly = stall; angle, not speed, causes it
Root-first stallStall begins at the root (rectangular wings do this naturally)Ailerons keep working; buffet warning — the pattern designers want
Tip-first stallTapered / swept wings tend to stall at the tipsWing drop, ailerons in stalled air — fixed with washout, stall strips, tip slots
Vortex generatorsSmall fins that stir high-energy air into the boundary layerFlow stays attached longer → lower stall speed, better control

Flow regimes

RegimeMachWhat is happeningDrag
Subsonicbelow McritAll airflow over the wing below Mach 1Normal drag curve
Critical Mach≈ 0.8Air over the wing first touches Mach 1Shock waves about to form
Transonic≈ 0.8 – 1.3Mixed flow; shocks on top, then bottom; shock stall possibleWave drag — rises sharply past Mcrd
Supersonicabove ≈ 1.3All flow supersonic; bow wave or attached oblique shockHigh but steadier; sharp thin wings help

Designing for it: sweepback lowers the effective Mach along the chord (higher Mcrit); thin wings with sharp leading edges turn the bow wave into an attached oblique shock. Example: Lockheed F-104 Starfighter — first US fighter to fly at twice the speed of sound.

Four quick calculators

Units

Knots to km/h

km/h = kt × 1.852
ktknots — nautical miles per hour1.852kilometres in one nautical mile

A nautical mile is one minute of latitude, which is why aviation and navigation use it.

Going the other way: km/h ÷ 1.852 = knots. One knot is also about 1.15 mph.

Worked example

250 kt × 1.852 = 463 km/h. A 100 km/h ground speed is 100 ÷ 1.852 = 54 kt.

= 463 km/h · 288 mph
Glide

Glide distance

distance = height × glide ratio
heightheight above the ground (ft)glide ratiodistance forward per unit of height lost (= L/D at best glide)

Flown at best glide speed, a 10:1 aircraft covers 10 ft forward for every 1 ft down. Any other speed — faster or slower — gives less.

1 nautical mile = 6,076 ft. Wind changes the ground distance; the ratio is through the air.

Worked example

5,000 ft above the ground at a 9:1 glide ratio: 5,000 × 9 = 45,000 ft ≈ 7.4 NM in still air.

= 45,000 ft · 7.4 NM
Temperature

Speed of sound

a ≈ 38.97 × √T
aspeed of sound in knotsTair temperature in kelvin (°C + 273)

Only temperature matters. Warm molecules pass a pressure wave along faster; cold ones slower. Altitude only matters because it is colder up high.

ISA sea level 15 °C → 661 kt (761 mph). ISA 35,000 ft, about −54 °C → 575 kt.

Worked example

At −54 °C: T = 219 K, a = 38.97 × √219 = 577 kt. At 15 °C: 38.97 × √288 = 661 kt.

a = 661 kt · 761 mph
High-speed flight

Mach number

M = TAS ÷ a
MMach numberTAStrue airspeed (kt)alocal speed of sound (kt) — from the temperature

The same true airspeed is a higher Mach number in cold air. That is why airliners watch the Mach meter up high, not just the airspeed.

Critical Mach (about 0.8 for many wings) is where the air over the wing — not the aircraft — first hits Mach 1.

Worked example

470 kt true at −54 °C (a ≈ 577 kt): M = 470 ÷ 577 = 0.81. The same 470 kt at sea level on a 15 °C day: 470 ÷ 661 = 0.71.

M = 0.81

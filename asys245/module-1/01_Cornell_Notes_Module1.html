Atmosphere

What is dry air made of?

Composition
  • Dry air ≈ 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, ~1% other gases (argon, CO₂).
  • "Dry" = water vapour not counted. Real air also carries moisture.
  • Air is a gas: it has no fixed shape, it can be squeezed, and it expands when heated.

Almost 4 of every 5 molecules you breathe are nitrogen.

Go deeper

Your notes say 20% oxygen; the standard figure used in ground school (and in From the Ground Up) is 21%. The mix stays the same all the way up — what changes with altitude is how much air there is, not what it is made of. That is why pilots and engines struggle at altitude: same 21% oxygen, but far fewer molecules per breath or per cylinder.

Gas (Air)

Which four properties describe the state of a gas?

State of a gas
  • Pressure (p) – how hard the gas pushes on its container.
  • Temperature (T) – how fast the molecules move.
  • Mass (m) – how much gas there is.
  • Volume (V) – the space that contains the gas.
  • Together these four determine the state of the gas. Change one, and at least one other changes.
Go deeper

The gas laws below each hold one property steady and show how two of the others trade off. Boyle's Law holds temperature steady (pressure vs volume). Charles' Law holds pressure steady (volume vs temperature). Density ties mass and volume together.

Boyle's Law

If you squeeze a gas into a smaller volume, what happens to its pressure?

Temp constant
p × V = C
  • p = pressure, V = volume, C = a constant (temperature does not change).
  • Pressure and volume are inversely related: V ↑ = p ↓ and V ↓ = p ↑.
  • Example: if pV = 10, then 2 × 5 = 10 or 5 × 2 = 10. Double the pressure → volume halves.
  • Demo: cap a syringe and push the plunger — smaller volume, the air pushes back harder (SciGuys Boyle's Law video, balloon-in-syringe).

Same air, half the space → more than twice the pushing. p × V stays 10.

Go deeper

"Inversely" (a Tier 2 word) means: when one goes up, the other goes down by the same factor. Boyle's Law is why a sealed bag of chips puffs up in an aircraft cabin at altitude — cabin pressure drops, so the air trapped in the bag expands. It also explains why your ears "pop" on climb and descent.

Charles' Law

If you heat a gas at constant pressure, what happens to its volume?

Law of Volumes · Pressure constant
V = c × T
  • At constant pressure, volume is proportional to temperature: T ↑ = V ↑, T ↓ = V ↓.
  • c is the constant (the pressure held steady).
  • Warm air spreads out; cold air shrinks. A balloon in a freezer goes limp; in the sun it swells.

Boyle = curve (inverse). Charles = straight line (proportional). Know the difference.

Go deeper

"Proportional" (Tier 2) means both change together in the same direction, by the same ratio: double the temperature (measured in kelvin) and you double the volume. This is the physics behind hot-air balloons — heat the air inside, it expands and becomes less dense than the air outside, so the balloon rises.

Density

What is density, and why do aircraft and engines perform better in cold air?

D = m ÷ V
D = m / V
  • Density = mass divided by volume — how much "stuff" is packed into a space. (Rearranged: V = m / D.)
  • Temperature chain: ↑ Temp = ↑ Volume = ↓ Density and ↓ Temp = ↓ Volume = ↑ Density.
  • Denser air (cold air) = more efficient aircraft and better performance of plane and engine: more lift from the wing, more thrust from the prop, more oxygen per stroke in the engine.

Same mass, bigger volume, lower density. A wing "grabs" fewer molecules per second in warm air.

Go deeper

This is why a hot summer afternoon at a high-elevation airport is the worst case for take-off: hot air is thin, so the aircraft needs a longer run and climbs poorly. Pilots call this "high density altitude" — the air behaves as if the runway were much higher than it really is. Medicine Hat at 2,350 ft on a 30 °C day feels to the wing like an airport well above 4,000 ft.

Air Pressure

What is air pressure, and why does it drop as you climb?

Weight of air
  • Air pressure = the weight of the air above a given location.
  • ↑ altitude = ↓ air — there is less air above you than there was at lower altitude, so less weight pressing down.
  • ↓ pressure = ↑ gas volume — with less pressure on it, a gas expands (Boyle's Law again).

Pressure is the weight of the column above you. Shorter column, less weight, lower pressure.

Go deeper

Pressure drops roughly 1 inHg for every 1,000 ft in the lower atmosphere. So Medicine Hat (2,350 ft) sits at about 27.5 inHg on a standard day instead of 29.92. Half of all the atmosphere's air is below about 18,000 ft — which is why cabins are pressurized and oxygen is required at height.

International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)

What are the "standard day" numbers at sea level, and why do we need a standard?

Standard day
  • Standard day at sea level: pressure 14.7 psi = 29.92 inHg = 1013.2 mb (hPa); surface temperature 15 °C (59 °F).
  • All three pressure values are the same pressure in different units. Know all three.
  • Why sea level? It is reproducible and consistent across the world — a reference everyone can compare to. Real weather is always compared against this standard.
  • Local tie-in: Medicine Hat airport altitude = 2,350 ft above sea level, so MH is never at "sea level" conditions.
Go deeper

Check your notes: 15 °C converts to 59 °F, not 52 °F (°F = °C × 1.8 + 32). ISA also sets a standard temperature drop of about 2 °C per 1,000 ft. On a standard day Medicine Hat would be about 15 − (2.35 × 2) ≈ 10 °C. Aircraft performance charts and altimeters are all built on ISA, which is why the number 29.92 will follow you through your whole career.

Bernoulli's Principle

What happens to pressure when a fluid speeds up, and how does an airfoil use this?

Total = Dynamic + Static
  • When the velocity of a fluid through a constricted tube is increased, the pressure within the fluid is decreased.
  • A × V = constant — narrow the area, the fluid must speed up (continuity).
  • Total pressure = Dynamic pressure + Static pressure
    Total stays the same, so when dynamic (speed) goes up, static (push on the surface) goes down.
  • Shape of the airfoil is because of Bernoulli: air over the curved top travels faster → ↑ velocity = ↓ pressure on top → higher pressure underneath pushes the wing up = lift.

Top: the venturi tube — narrow section, faster flow, lower pressure. Bottom: the wing's curved top is a half-venturi.

Go deeper

Bernoulli explains part of lift; the wing's angle to the airflow (angle of attack) deflecting air downward is the other part — you will meet that in Module 2 (Lift and Drag). Bernoulli also runs your instruments: the airspeed indicator measures dynamic pressure (total − static) through the pitot tube, and carburetors use a venturi to draw fuel into the airstream.

Summary

Air is a gas of mostly nitrogen and oxygen whose state is set by pressure, temperature, mass and volume. Boyle's Law (p × V = C) says squeezing a gas raises its pressure; Charles' Law (V = cT) says heating it makes it expand. Because density is mass ÷ volume, warm air is thin and cold air is dense — and dense air gives a wing and an engine more to work with. Air pressure is the weight of the air above you, so it falls as you climb; the International Standard Atmosphere fixes a "standard day" at sea level (14.7 psi / 29.92 inHg / 1013.2 hPa, 15 °C) so every pilot and engineer measures against the same reference. Bernoulli's principle — faster fluid, lower pressure — is why an airfoil is curved on top: faster air above, lower pressure, lift.

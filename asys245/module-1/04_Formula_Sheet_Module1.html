Gas laws

Boyle's Law

Pressure ↔ Volume

p × V = C
ppressureVvolumeCa constant

Squeeze a gas into less space and its pressure goes up. Inverse: one up, the other down.

Held constant: temperature. Graph: curve.

Worked example

pV = 10. If p = 2 then V = 5: 2 × 5 = 10. If p = 5 then V = 2: 5 × 2 = 10. Double the pressure → half the volume.

Two states, same temperature: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
V₂ = 2.5
Charles' Law · Law of Volumes

Volume ↔ Temperature

V = c × T
VvolumeTtemperature (kelvin for the math)ca constant

Heat a gas and it expands; cool it and it shrinks. Proportional: both move together.

Held constant: pressure. Graph: straight line.

Worked example

A balloon is 2.0 L at 20 °C (293 K). Warm it to 40 °C (313 K): V₂ = 2.0 × 313 ÷ 293 = 2.14 L. Note: use kelvin (°C + 273), or "double the temperature" gives the wrong answer.

V₁ ÷ T₁ = V₂ ÷ T₂ (T in °C, converted to kelvin for you)
V₂ = 2.14
Density

Mass ÷ Volume

D = m / V
DdensitymmassVvolume  (rearranged: V = m / D)

How tightly packed the air is. Same mass in a bigger space = lower density.

Chain to remember: ↑ Temp → ↑ Volume → ↓ Density. Dense (cold) air = better aircraft and engine performance.

Worked example

1.2 kg of air fills 1.0 m³: D = 1.2 ÷ 1.0 = 1.2 kg/m³ (about sea-level density). Warm it so it fills 1.1 m³: 1.2 ÷ 1.1 = 1.09 kg/m³ — thinner air.

D = 1.20

Airflow · Bernoulli

Continuity

Area × Velocity

A × V = constant
Across-section area of the tubeVvelocity of the fluid

Narrow the tube and the fluid must speed up to keep the same flow going through.

This is step 1 of the venturi. Bernoulli is step 2.

Worked example

Air enters at 20 m/s through area 4. The throat has area 2: 4 × 20 = 2 × V₂ → V₂ = 40 m/s. Half the area, twice the speed.

V₂ = 40
Bernoulli's Principle

Total Pressure

Total = Dynamic + Static
Totalstays the same along the flowDynamicpressure from motion — rises with speedStaticpressure pressing on the surface

Faster fluid → more dynamic → less static. Speed up, pressure drops.

Why the airfoil is curved on top: faster air above → lower static pressure → lift. Why the airspeed indicator works: it reads Total − Static = Dynamic.

Worked example

Total pressure is 1000. Slow air: dynamic 100, so static 1000 − 100 = 900. Over the wing the air speeds up: dynamic 300, so static 1000 − 300 = 700. Lower static pressure on top → lift.

Static = 700

Conversions · altitude

Temperature

°C ↔ °F

°F = °C × 1.8 + 32

Reverse: °C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8. Kelvin = °C + 273.

Check: 15 °C → 59 °F. 0 °C → 32 °F. −40 is the same in both.

→ °F 59.0
→ °C 15.0
Pressure units

psi · inHg · hPa

14.7 psi = 29.92 inHg = 1013.2 hPa

Three units, one pressure. 1 inHg ≈ 33.86 hPa ≈ 0.491 psi.

Altimeters use inHg (Canada/US) or hPa (most of the world). 1 mb = 1 hPa.

= 1013.2 hPa = 14.70 psi
ISA with altitude (rule of thumb)

Pressure & temp drop as you climb

−1 inHg & −2 °C per 1,000 ft

Less air above you → lower pressure. Standard temperature also falls with height.

Works well in the lower atmosphere (below ~10,000 ft). Medicine Hat at 2,350 ft on a standard day: about 27.6 inHg and 10 °C.

Worked example

Medicine Hat, 2,350 ft: 29.92 − 2.35 = 27.57 inHg; 15 − (2 × 2.35) = 10.3 °C.

Pressure ≈ 27.57 inHg   Temp ≈ 10.3 °C
